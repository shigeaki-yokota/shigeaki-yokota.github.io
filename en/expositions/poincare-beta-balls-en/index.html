Question | Which distance multiplier leaves measurable information as the dimension grows

Let the dimension nnn grow and vary the multiplier applied to distances. The behavior of measurements then splits in two. If the multiplier is small, every measurement is nearly constant (concentration). If it is large, sets carrying prescribed probabilities can be pushed far apart (dissipation). The multiplier lying between the two is the critical multiplier. The question is: for beta-type probabilities on the Poincaré ball, what survives in the limit at the critical multiplier?

The answer splits into four critical-limit regimes: a railway pyramid carrying shifted exponential laws, the family of spaces of diameter at most one, a nonlinear Gaussian transform, and the Gaussian. In the railway regime both the width of the radius and the separation between directions survive. The other three regimes use a representative shell, but in the Gaussian regime a Gaussian coordinate for the radius must be retained as well. What completes the classification is the upper inclusion for the railway pyramid, which controls every finite bounded observation, together with the sharp condition for the limit to be contained in the diameter-at-most-one family (Introduction, Table 1, Theorems 1.1 and 5.1).

Explainer's note. Here is the whole picture first. Scatter points at random inside a ball and measure them in the hyperbolic metric. The parameter β\betaβ decides how strongly the points are pushed toward the boundary. As nnn grows and β=βn\beta=\beta_nβ=βn​ moves, the limit, once distances are rescaled, takes the following four shapes in turn.

  1. βn\beta_nβn​ very small (βnlog⁡n\beta_n\log nβn​logn finite). The points scatter very close to the boundary and the width of the radius survives. The limit is a tree with branches leaving a center, a railway space.
  2. βn\beta_nβn​ somewhat larger. The width of the radius disappears, but slight differences in angle strongly affect the distance. The limit is "all spaces of diameter at most one".
  3. βn\beta_nβn​ of order n\sqrt nn​. The metric transform survives as it is, and the limit is a nonlinearly distorted Gaussian.
  4. βn\beta_nβn​ much larger than n\sqrt nn​. The limit is the Gaussian itself.

What follows first fixes the probability space, then prepares the tools for comparing sequences of spaces, and then builds the railway space that appears in the limit. After that the four regimes are tabulated, the proofs for the regimes are followed one by one, and finally the sharpness of the boundary to the diameter-at-most-one regime is traced.

What are the axioms of a pyramid, and the definition of weak convergence through spaces?

An mm-space has a complete separable metric space, a Borel probability, and the whole space as the support. The support is the set of points every neighborhood of which has positive probability. Spaces that are isometric by a probability-preserving map are identified (Definitions 2.1–2.2). Y≺XY\prec XY≺X means that a measure-preserving 1-Lipschitz map X→YX\to YX→Y exists.

Result | Angular amplification and radial width determine the four critical limits

The railway pyramid was the limit of the first of the four regimes. Together with the remaining three, we now introduce the quantities that separate the regimes and tabulate the classification.

Two knobs determine the regime. One is how much differences of angle are amplified into distance (A\mathcal AA). How does this differ from the Gaussian limit of the sphere and from earlier work?

The Gaussian pyramid limit of the sphere is Shioya's theorem, which this paper uses after adjusting to the needed normalization in Remark 3.14. The Gaussian limit of ellipsoids is due to Kazukawa–Shioya, and the half-line limit of Cauchy-type distributions by a cone construction to Esaki–Kazukawa–Mitsuishi (Introduction; Theorems 1.1 and 1.2 of reference [4]; Theorems 1.2 and 1.4 of [1]).

In this paper a rotation-symmetric probability in Euclidean coordinates is measured by the hyperbolic metric, so angular amplification and radial width must be kept at once. In particular, the upper inclusion recovering all finite bounded observations in the railway regime, and the necessity and sufficiency of the condition for convergence to the diameter-at-most-one regime, complete the classification into four critical-limit regimes (Theorems 1.1 and 5.1, Introduction).

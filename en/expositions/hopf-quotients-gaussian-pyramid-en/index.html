Does forgetting sign remain different from forgetting phase in the limit as dimension grows? Can we still distinguish these two quotients and the original Gaussian space even if we allow a change in the unit of distance? Theorem A proves that the three objects are pairwise non-similar. To make this precise, we first introduce pyramids, which describe the limits.

The hardest part of the proof is comparing the two quotients. Computing two quantities that measure variation and tails first distinguishes the original Gaussian space from its quotients. But the values coincide for the two quotients, so we turn to a different question: can we extract the half-line carrying the half-normal distribution? Assuming that this half-line belongs to the limit, we approximate the squares of observables by quadratic forms and obtain a third moment incompatible with phase symmetry.

Collecting the observations obtainable from a space

A space equipped with distance and probability is called an mm-space: more precisely, a complete separable metric space XXX together with a Borel probability measure μX\mu_XμX​. Completeness means that every Cauchy sequence—a sequence whose terms eventually become arbitrarily close to one another—has a limit in the space. Separability means that a countable set of points can approximate every point of the space to arbitrary accuracy. A Borel probability measure assigns probabilities to the sets generated from open sets by countable unions, complements, and related operations, with μX(X)=1\mu_X(X)=1μX​(X)=1. We work on the support of the measure, the set of points every neighborhood of which has positive probability. Write X≺YX\prec YX≺Y when there is a map f:Y→Xf:Y\to Xf:Y→X that does not increase distance, meaning

and preserves probability, meaning f∗μY=μXf_*\mu_Y=\mu_Xf∗​μY​=μX​. This says that the distance and probability structure of XXX can be extracted from YYY without amplifying distances. For example, the map taking the first coordinate of Γn\Gamma^nΓn gives Γ1≺Γn\Gamma^1\prec\Gamma^nΓ1≺Γn. More generally, a function is Lipschitz if the difference of its values is at most LLL times the distance for some finite constant LLL; the smallest such LLL is its Lipschitz constant. Pyramids provide a framework for handling such collections. In general, a pyramid is a nonempty family of mm-spaces, identified up to probability-preserving bijective isometries, satisfying the following three conditions. An isometry is a map preserving the distance between every pair of points.

  • Every space obtainable by ≺\prec≺ from a member of the family also belongs to the family.
  • Any two members can be obtained from a single common member of the family.
  • Taking a limit in the box distance keeps us inside the family.

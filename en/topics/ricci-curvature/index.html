Topics / Geometry / A first look at curvature

What does Ricci curvature add up?

Ricci curvature adds the sectional curvatures transverse to a chosen direction of travel. But what is negative in negative curvature? Separating the curvature of a curve, the two principal bendings of a surface, and sectional curvature reveals what the Ricci sum means.

1 / Begin with curves

Curvature measures how fast direction changes as you move

Walking along a straight line does not change direction. Along a circle, the same distance walked changes direction more on a smaller circle. Curvature measures this change of direction per unit distance.

A circle of radius R has curvature 1/R. A large circle looks nearly straight, so its curvature is small. A small circle turns sharply and has larger curvature.

Curvature = change of direction / distance traveled; for a circle, κ = 1/R

Define the ratio by a limit as distance becomes arbitrarily small. For distance Δs and change of unit tangent ΔT, consider |ΔT|/Δs and its limit as the step shrinks. Parametrize a smooth curve by arc length s; its unit tangent T(s) is the direction of travel, of length 1. Curvature magnitude is κ(s)=|dT/ds|, where the derivative denotes that rate of change. Around a circle, the tangent turns through angle 2π over arc length 2πR, giving 1/R.

Here κ=|dT/ds| is a magnitude, so it cannot be negative. A signed curvature can be defined by choosing a normal and calling bending toward that normal positive. Bending the other way is negative. This describes bending relative to the chosen normal.

Distinguish three quantities:
  • Curve curvature magnitude κ is nonnegative. Signed curvature has a sign relative to a chosen normal.
  • Surface normal curvature k(v) in one direction also has a sign relative to a chosen surface normal.
  • Sectional curvature belongs to a two-dimensional plane in the tangent space, not to a single direction alone.

Thus negativity in one direction can describe bending relative to a curve's or surface's normal. But however a one-dimensional curve is drawn in space, its own distance gives zero Riemannian curvature, and it has no two-dimensional plane on which to define sectional curvature. In the surface examples, keep track of which quantity is negative.

2 / Surfaces in three dimensions

A surface has two principal ways to bend

A surface has many directions at a point. Magnify a neighborhood and consider the plane touching the surface with the same slope: the tangent plane. Under magnification, a smooth surface approaches this plane. A unit vector perpendicular to it is a normal. Choose a tangent direction and cut the surface by the plane spanned by that direction and the normal. The resulting plane curve's signed bending toward the normal is the surface's normal curvature in that direction.

Choose one side's unit normal; for the paraboloids below it points upward. As the tangent direction turns, normal curvature varies. Two perpendicular directions attaining its maximum and minimum are principal directions, and their values are k₁,k₂. Reversing the normal reverses both signs but leaves their product K=k₁k₂ unchanged. These two numbers give the first local description of shape.

To see why these directions exist, align the tangent plane with the xy plane and write the surface locally as z=f(x,y). Choose coordinates so height and slope vanish at the point. With our sign convention, the second derivative in a direction gives its normal curvature there: it measures change of slope. For z=x²−y² at the origin, the x direction bends upward and the y direction downward. The second-derivative matrix [[fxx,fxy],[fxy,fyy]] is symmetric, since smooth functions satisfy fxy=fyx. Linear algebra diagonalizes a symmetric matrix in perpendicular axes. Those axes are the principal directions and the diagonal entries are k₁,k₂.

On an ordinary sphere, both principal directions bend to the same side. At a saddle's center, one direction is bowl-shaped and its perpendicular is hill-shaped: opposite bendings.

3 / Distances within the surface

A cylinder looks curved but has zero Gaussian curvature

Viewed in three-dimensional space, a cylinder bends. But rolling a sheet of paper into a cylinder leaves lengths and angles measured on the paper unchanged. Cutting it lengthwise and unrolling restores a locally flat sheet.

Gaussian curvature does not simply count bending seen by an outside observer. It detects curvature a tiny surveyor living on the surface can find by measuring distances along it. A cylinder has no local distortion of these distances, hence zero Gaussian curvature.

Another test is a small triangle whose sides are shortest surface paths. For a geodesic triangle, the angle sum minus its flat value is the integral of curvature over its interior. Area integration divides the triangle into tiny pieces and adds curvature times area; dA denotes area. Angles use radians, with π radians equal to 180°.

Angle sum − π = ∫triangle K dA

Positive Gaussian curvature gives a small triangle angle sum greater than π; negative curvature gives less. A small cylinder triangle becomes a plane triangle when unrolled.

The distinction: Principal curvatures k₁,k₂ depend on embedding in ambient three-dimensional space. Their product K is determined by distances within the surface. Looking bent and being intrinsically curved are different properties.

4 / From a surface sum to higher dimensions

One term on a surface, two terms in three dimensions

On a surface the tangent space is already a two-dimensional plane. For unit v, there is just one perpendicular unit direction e, up to sign. Together they span the whole tangent plane, whose sectional curvature is Gaussian curvature K. The Ricci sum has one term: Ric(v,v)=K.

We mean Ric(v,v) for a unit direction v. Starting with surfaces makes “sum of sectional curvatures” begin with one term on a surface; higher dimension adds transverse planes.

In dimensions three and above, more tangent directions leave a point p. Choose a unit direction v of travel. The curvature of each plane containing it is sectional curvature.

In three dimensions the perpendicular complement of v is a plane. Choose perpendicular unit basis vectors e₁,e₂, and examine the curvatures of the planes spanned by v,e₁ and v,e₂.

In n dimensions, the perpendicular complement of unit v has dimension n−1. Choose perpendicular unit vectors e₂,…,eₙ there and add the curvatures of their planes with v:

Ric(v,v) = K(v,e₂)+K(v,e₃)+⋯+K(v,eₙ),　|v|=1.

What is “counted” is the sum of sectional curvatures over all perpendicular transverse directions, rather than a number of points or curves. Dividing by n−1 gives mean sectional curvature. The usual Ricci tensor uses the sum, not that mean.

5 / From diagrams to definitions

Define the sectional-curvature sum as a trace

The terminology below describes stages of the same construction: choose a plane, measure its curvature, and compare it with the other planes.

A Riemannian manifold is a smooth space locally described by n real coordinates, with an inner product g smoothly specifying lengths and angles at each point. The vector space of possible directions at p is the tangent space. Its inner product gives lengths |X|=√g(X,X) and orthogonality.

Vector fields Y,Z assign a vector to each point. Acting on a function h, a field X gives its directional rate of change X(h). If X points right in the plane and h=x is horizontal position, X(h)=1. Differentiating a vector field along X examines how it changes after moving slightly. On a surface, discard the component normal to the surface. The corresponding operation on a general space is a connection, written ∇XY. Compatibility with the inner product means X(g(Y,Z))=g(∇XY,Z)+g(Y,∇XZ). Being torsion-free means ∇XY−∇YX=[X,Y]: the difference of these derivatives agrees with the directional commutator. Exactly one connection satisfies both conditions, the Levi-Civita connection.

The curvature tensor compares derivatives of Z in the orders X,Y and Y,X. The directions themselves may vary, requiring the final correction ∇[X,Y]Z. Coordinate direction fields satisfy [X,Y]=0. We choose the convention making sphere curvature positive:

R(X,Y)Z = ∇X∇YZ − ∇Y∇XZ − ∇[X,Y]Z.

The commutator is defined on functions by [X,Y](h)=X(Y(h))−Y(X(h)), comparing the two derivative orders. For example, if X points right and Y points north with magnitude proportional to horizontal position x, moving right increases the northward field and [X,Y] points north. Coordinate fields in the same chart commute. The correction subtracts changes of the direction fields themselves. The curvature tensor also records rotation from parallel transport around an infinitesimal loop.

Sectional curvature

Let perpendicular unit vectors u,w span a two-dimensional plane σ in the tangent space at p. Define

K(σ) = K(u,w) = g(R(u,w)w,u).

For arbitrary independent u,w, divide the right side by g(u,u)g(w,w)−g(u,w)², the squared area of their parallelogram. The result depends only on σ, not on its basis.

Ricci curvature (Ricci tensor)

For an orthonormal tangent basis e₁,…,eₙ at p, define

Ric(X,Y) = Σi=1n g(R(eᵢ,X)Y,eᵢ).

For unit v choose e₁=v. Curvature symmetries give g(R(eᵢ,v)v,eᵢ)=g(R(v,eᵢ)eᵢ,v), whose right side is the defining sectional curvature. Therefore

Ric(v,v) = Σi=2n K(v,eᵢ).

Equal first two inputs make the curvature tensor vanish, so the travel direction's own term is zero. The remaining terms are the planes paired with perpendicular directions.

Why does a different orthonormal basis give the same answer? Matrix trace, the sum of diagonal entries, is unchanged by orthonormal basis changes. Ricci takes the trace of a curvature operator, so any perpendicular basis gives the same sum. For a two-by-two matrix, trace is a+d. This combines many sectional curvatures into one value for each direction.

Check the sign on a sphere

A standard sphere of radius a has sectional curvature K=1/a² on every tangent two-plane. In dimension n,

Ric(v,v) = (n−1)/a²　(|v|=1).

For a three-dimensional sphere of radius 2, every sectional curvature is 1/4. Two transverse directions give Ricci curvature 1/4+1/4=1/2. Our convention is positive on spheres.

Write the trace in linear algebra notation

Fix X and consider A_X:Z↦R(Z,X)X. For unit X, Ric(X,X)=tr(A_X). Taking trace in an orthonormal basis yields exactly the sectional-curvature sum. Its symmetric bilinear extension to arbitrary X,Y is the Ricci tensor defined above.

6 / Why a transverse sum?

The sum appears in the volume of a geodesic bundle

Send geodesics from p at equal speeds in slightly different directions. A geodesic generalizes a locally shortest surface path. The displacement of neighboring paths from a reference path is described by a vector field called a Jacobi field.

First in two dimensions, fix the initial angular spread and distance traveled. Measure transverse width along the geodesic circle of radius r between two paths starting at the same point. In the plane it grows proportionally to r; curvature changes that rate.

Here j(r) is width per unit initial angle. An angular spread Δθ has arc length j(r)Δθ along the constant-curvature geodesic circle. This is not the shortest distance between its endpoints.

The three functions are exact solutions of the two-dimensional constant-curvature Jacobi equation j''+Kj=0, with j(0)=0 and j'(0)=1. The second derivative measures change of spreading speed. For positive curvature j''=−j slows it; for negative curvature j''=j accelerates it. Even positive curvature gives growing width for small r, not immediate contraction of all distances. This illustrates the local mechanism and does not prove finite-distance behavior on a general manifold. See Ohta, §2, equations (2.1)–(2.2) and §3.1, equation (3.1).

For a unit-speed reference geodesic γ(r) and transverse Jacobi field J(r),

Dr²J + R(J,γ′)γ′ = 0,　J(0)=0.

Dr is covariant differentiation along the geodesic. Once initial spreading DrJ(0) is specified, curvature determines its evolution. One transverse direction made this vector equation a scalar width equation in the experiment.

Higher dimension has more transverse directions and a transverse volume. Multiplying their spreads makes the first curvature correction a sum of sectional curvatures: Ric(v,v)=ΣK(v,eᵢ).

For volume comparison, consider a tiny directional neighborhood of unit v, then let its angular width tend to zero and measure cross-sectional volume per unit angular size at distance r. Divide this density by the flat density rn−1. For a bundle of finite angular width, the first r² correction averages Ricci curvature over its directions; higher corrections are not determined by that average alone. Here shrinking angular width selects the central direction v:

Volume density ratio = 1 − Ric(v,v) r²/6 + O(r³)　(r→0).

This is the local geodesic-polar expansion from p in direction v. The remainder O(r³) has absolute value bounded by a constant times r³ for sufficiently small r. It gives a quadratic change near the origin, not complete finite-distance volume from one Ricci value. If Ric(v,v)>0, small-distance bundle volume begins smaller than in flat space.

Derive the coefficient 1/6 and the Ricci sum from products of widths

First follow one transverse displacement on a surface. Vary the initial direction of a reference geodesic and travel the same small distance r. Endpoints lie on a small geodesic circle centered at p. Their variation is tangent to the circle, while γ′(r) is radial. Gauss's lemma gives their orthogonality. More concretely, the derivative of distance in direction V is g(V,γ′(r)), the radial component. Motion on the circle leaves distance unchanged, so that component is zero. The Jacobi field J(r) is thus perpendicular to γ′(r). On a surface there is only one such tangent direction.

Parallel-transport a unit vector E(r) in this perpendicular direction. Write J(r)=j(r)E(r), with j(r) the signed displacement for unit initial angular variation. The curvature operator R(E,γ′)γ′ is perpendicular with coefficient g(R(E,γ′)γ′,E)=K(r), the surface's Gaussian curvature. In this parallel frame the Jacobi equation becomes the ordinary differential equation j''(r)+K(r)j(r)=0.

All paths meet initially, so j(0)=0; normalize j'(0)=1. Substitution gives j''(0)=0. Differentiating, j'''(r)+K'(r)j(r)+K(r)j'(r)=0, by the product rule (Kj)'=K'j+Kj'. Thus j'''(0)=−K(0). Successive derivatives describe the rate of change and its subsequent changes.

A Taylor expansion approximates a smooth function near a point by a polynomial matching its derivatives. For a₀+a₁r+a₂r²+a₃r³, the value and first three derivatives at zero are a₀,a₁,2a₂,6a₃. Hence the cubic approximation is j(0)+j'(0)r+j''(0)r²/2+j'''(0)r³/6. For example, differentiating 5r³ three times gives 5×3×2×1=30, recovering its coefficient as 30/6=5.

Substitution gives j(r)=r−K(0)r³/6+O(r⁴), where the remainder is bounded by a constant times r⁴. Relative to flat spread r, j(r)/r=1−K(0)r²/6+O(r³). In higher dimension, corresponding transverse corrections multiply to give volume.

Bundle Jacobi fields into a matrix; the determinant gives small transverse volume. The transverse curvature operator is symmetric, so choose at p an orthonormal eigenbasis e₁,…,eₙ₋₁ and transport it parallelly. The second-order length correction for axis i is −Kᵢr²/6, with Kᵢ=K(v,eᵢ) at the starting point. The operator need not remain diagonal along the path, but the starting operator determines this order of Taylor correction. Thus the product (1−K₁r²/6)(1−K₂r²/6)⋯ contributes −(K₁+⋯+Kₙ₋₁)r²/6. The sum is the curvature operator's trace, equal to Ric(v,v). Ricci therefore governs the first curvature correction to transverse volume around one geodesic.

The opening question answered: Ricci sums sectional curvatures over perpendicular transverse directions. Their total describes how nearby geodesics' cross-sectional volume first deviates from flat behavior.

7 / From pointwise curvature to a uniform condition

A Ricci lower bound is a floor at every point and in every direction

If Ricci curvature never drops below a constant K₀, as point p and unit direction v vary, this is a Ricci lower bound: Ricp(v,v)≥K₀. For arbitrary tangent vectors it reads

Ricp(X,X) ≥ K₀ |X|²　(for every point p and tangent vector X).

This is a uniform minimum, not positivity at one location. A two-dimensional sphere of radius a has Ric(v,v)=1/a², so any K₀≤1/a² is a lower bound. A plane or cylinder has Ric=0. If a saddle center has Ric=-1, no bound K₀>-1 can hold on an entire space containing it.

This differs from bounding every sectional curvature below.

If every two-plane has sectional curvature at least κ, summing n−1 terms yields Ric≥(n−1)κg. The converse generally fails. A sum can stay positive despite negative sections if others compensate. Try −2+3=1 in the three-dimensional sliders.

A Ricci bound in comparison geometry concerns more than the appearance of one surface point. As points and travel directions vary, it uniformly restricts how much geodesic bundles and balls can expand.

8 / In the language of distance and measure

The CD condition expresses Ricci bounds through transport of probability clouds

So far a geodesic was a point path γ:[0,1]→M. A shortest constant-speed path from γ(0)=x to γ(1)=y satisfies d(γ(s),γ(t))=|s−t|d(x,y). This is a path of points within the space.

Now let probability distributions move. Measures μ₀,μ₁ on M are probability clouds of total mass 1. Write P(X) for probability measures on X. To define distance W₂, consider a coupling π describing how much mass goes from x to y and minimize total squared-distance cost. The projections are p₀(x,y)=x, p₁(x,y)=y on M×M:

π∈P(M×M),　(p₀)#π=μ₀,　(p₁)#π=μ₁
W₂²(μ₀,μ₁)=infπ satisfying these marginals ∫M×Md(x,y)² dπ(x,y).

The coupling is a mass correspondence. Mass need not share a single destination, and mass from one point may split between destinations. Write P₂(M) for probabilities with finite second moment ∫d(o,x)²dμ(x) from a reference point o. On this class W₂ is a finite metric.

Let Γ(M) collect all shortest constant-speed geodesics γ:[0,1]→M with varying endpoints. On a complete Riemannian manifold, Hopf–Rinow provides one between every pair of points. Lift an optimal correspondence π to a probability Π on endpoint-joining paths, whose endpoint-pair distribution is π. For compact length spaces, existence of such a lift is Lott–Villani v4, §2.2, Proposition 2.6. A general metric-measure CD condition includes existence of a suitable Π among its requirements. Reading position at time t by et(γ)=γ(t), set

π=(e₀,e₁)#Π,　μt=(et)#Π.

The notation F#ν is push-forward: (F#ν)(A)=ν(F⁻¹(A)). Thus π=(e₀,e₁)#Π describes endpoint pairs and μt=(et)#Π describes positions at time t.
Here are the two geodesics: each γ is an ordinary geodesic in M; collecting all positions by time gives t↦μt, a geodesic in the measure space (P₂(M),W₂). The latter is a W₂-geodesic, or displacement interpolation.

Why is this measure path shortest and constant-speed? Pairing the same particle at times s,t gives squared cost |t−s|²W₂(μ₀,μ₁)², hence W₂(μs,μt)≤|t−s|W₂(μ₀,μ₁). For 0≤s≤t≤1, apply the triangle inequality along μ₀→μs→μt→μ₁:

W₂(μ₀,μ₁) ≤ W₂(μ₀,μs)+W₂(μs,μt)+W₂(μt,μ₁)
≤ sD+(t−s)D+(1−t)D = D,　D:=W₂(μ₀,μ₁).

Both ends equal D, so every intermediate upper bound is equality. In particular W₂(μs,μt)=(t−s)D. Reverse the times for s>t. This proves shortest constant-speed motion in measure space.

Optimal couplings and W₂: Lott–Villani v4, §1.3. Distance estimates from pairing two times on one path: §2.1, Lemma 2.4. Existence of dynamical plans: §2.2, Proposition 2.6. Representation of arbitrary W₂-geodesics by dynamical plans: §2.3, Proposition 2.10.

Relative entropy

For μ=ρm, define Entm(μ)=∫ρ logρ dm to measure concentration relative to reference measure m. The density ρ is probability per unit reference measure. With this sign, greater concentration gives larger values.

For K₀=0, the entropy upper bound joins the endpoints by a straight line.

Blue is the inequality's right side, the allowed upper bound; dashes join the endpoints. Positive K₀ moves it below the line, negative K₀ above. It displays the condition's shape and does not calculate actual entropy.

Return to curvature. On a smooth manifold the transport paths x↦Ft(x) follow geodesics. Varying the starting point produces a Jacobi field, so Ricci controls their spreading. Mass conservation also gives ρt(Ft(x)) Jt(x)=ρ₀(x) almost everywhere, with Jt the transport Jacobian, its volume multiplier. Control volume through Jacobi fields, translate to density by mass conservation, then integrate to estimate entropy: this is the route from a Ricci bound to entropy convexity. The converse recovers small-scale volume change and Ricci bounds from inequalities for uniform measures on small balls. Ohta's Theorem 4.6 and its proof outline give the details.

For example, let the manifold be smooth, connected, complete, and without boundary, with m=volg. Choose compactly supported, finite-entropy absolutely continuous endpoint probabilities μᵢ=ρᵢm. Optimal transport and the W₂-geodesic are unique in this setting. Then Ric≥K₀g is equivalent to the following inequality for every such endpoint pair:

Entm(μt) ≤ (1−t)Entm(μ₀) + t Entm(μ₁) − (K₀/2)t(1−t)W₂(μ₀,μ₁)².
Proof route: turn Jacobi-field volume change into entropy

Here is the route from Ric≥K₀g to the displayed inequality on a smooth manifold. Separate control of pointwise volume multipliers from integration over all the mass.

  1. Represent transport by particle paths. In this setting optimal transport assigns a destination F₁(x) to almost every starting point. The map Ft gives the position on the geodesic from x to F₁(x). Thus γx(t)=Ft(x) is an ordinary particle geodesic and μt=(Ft)#μ₀ is the full W₂-geodesic.
  2. Study volume swept out by nearby paths. The Riemannian Jacobian Jt(x)=JacgFt(x) measures the local volume multiplier, with J₀=1. Varying the starting point gives Jacobi fields, putting curvature into its evolution. The comparison using the Ricci bound takes the following form as finite effective dimension N tends to infinity:
log Jt(x) ≥ (1−t)log J₀(x)+t log J₁(x)+(K₀/2)t(1−t)d(x,F₁(x))².

This is the N→∞ form of Ohta's finite-dimensional comparison (4.14). The differential comparison is the difficult part of the source proof; here we take it as an input and continue to density. A positive lower bound raises logarithmic volume multiplier above linear interpolation of its endpoints.

This does not contradict positive Ricci decreasing the volume of small geodesic balls relative to flat space. That compares a ball centered at one point with a flat ball. Here a transport map joining two distributions is compared to its own endpoint multipliers J₀,J₁. Different reference comparisons give the apparently different signs.

  1. Convert volume multipliers to density by mass conservation. Initial and transported regions contain the same mass. A volume multiplier Jt(x) divides density by that factor, giving the change-of-variables identity
ρt(Ft(x))Jt(x)=ρ₀(x).

Use it to change variables in the entropy integrals:

Entm(μt)=Entm(μ₀)−∫log Jt(x)dμ₀(x)
Entm(μ₁)=Entm(μ₀)−∫log J₁(x)dμ₀(x).
  1. Integrate the volume estimate over all mass. Substitution of the lower bound for log Jt reverses its sign and gives an entropy upper bound. The remaining distance integral is optimal transport cost: ∫d(x,F₁(x))²dμ₀(x)=W₂(μ₀,μ₁)². This yields the displayed CD(K₀,∞) inequality.

This is a proof map, not a complete proof. Actual optimal maps need not be smooth everywhere; differentiation and change of variables require almost-everywhere justification. The converse separately recovers local curvature from volume comparisons using distributions on small regions.

Source: Ohta v1, §4.2, the proof outline of Theorem 4.6, equations (4.13)–(4.14). We read (4.14) in its N→∞ limit.

Positive K₀ requires entropy to lie farther below linear interpolation. Negative K₀ raises the allowed bound and weakens the condition. Control of geodesic-bundle volume by a Ricci bound is thus expressed as an inequality for concentration and spreading of probability clouds.

For general CD conditions, at least one suitable path must exist. On general metric measure spaces, optimal transport and W₂-geodesics need not be unique. Lott–Villani require, for each endpoint pair, at least one optimal transport path satisfying convexity inequalities for a family of functionals determined by curvature and dimension. Relative entropy Entm is one example; finite N also involves Rényi-type functionals. We focus on the accessible entropy form. Some definitions require one path, while stronger versions require every path, so the source's quantifiers matter. For the smooth setting and absolutely continuous endpoints above, uniqueness removes this distinction. Even a fixed measure path μt need not uniquely determine its dynamical plan Π.
Finite-dimensional CD(K₀,N): N is an effective dimension upper bound, not necessarily an integer. One does not insert finite N into the same formula: a different inequality uses Rényi entropy and distance-dependent coefficients. On a complete smooth unweighted n-dimensional Riemannian manifold, N≥n and Ric≥K₀g correspond to CD(K₀,N). Formulating bounds with only distance and measure lets them persist when limits cease to be smooth manifolds.
How does finite-N entropy differ?

For μ=ρm, a typical finite-dimensional functional is Rényi entropy SN(μ)=−∫ρ1−1/Ndm. CD(K₀,N) controls it during transport with coefficients depending on K₀ and d(x,y). The precise definition requires the inequality along a suitable optimal transport path. Our slider shows the accessible CD(K₀,∞) form, not the finite-N definition.

CD(K₀,N) is a synthetic condition expressing lower curvature and upper dimension through metric and measure. We do not discuss RCD, which adds a Hilbertian energy condition.

Topics / Geometry / A first look at curvature

What does Ricci curvature add up?

Two people stand at the North Pole and set off due south along different meridians, each walking perfectly straight. At first they drift apart; past the equator they start closing in again, and they meet at the South Pole. On a flat plaza they would keep separating forever. Does a bundle of straight paths gather together, or spread out, compared with flat space? Ricci curvature condenses that tendency into one number for each direction of travel. This page builds it up in stages: the bending of a curve, the two bendings of a surface, the sectional curvatures of a space. We end with what Ricci curvature really is, the sum of the sectional curvatures transverse to the direction of travel, and why that sum describes the volume of a bundle of paths.

Starting here. You need vector lengths, inner products, and the ability to read function graphs. Derivatives are introduced as rates of change over very small steps. Connections and curvature tensors appear later, with their meanings and roles explained there. Each experiment opens with a question to predict before you touch anything, and ends with a “Check your prediction” panel. Committing to a guess first makes it much clearer what to look for.

1 / Begin with curves

Curvature measures how fast direction changes as you move

Every curvature of surfaces and spaces is ultimately assembled from how curves bend. So we start by turning the bending of a curve into a number, and by settling what the sign of that number means.

Walking along a straight line does not change direction. Along a circle, the same distance walked changes direction more on a smaller circle. Curvature measures this change of direction per unit distance. As an analogy, think of how far a car's steering wheel is turned: hold it at a fixed angle and the car drives in a circle, and the harder you turn it, the smaller the circle.

A circle of radius R has curvature 1/R. A large circle looks nearly straight, so its curvature is small. A small circle turns sharply and has larger curvature.

Curvature = change of direction / distance traveled; for a circle, κ = 1/R

Define the ratio by a limit as distance becomes arbitrarily small. For distance Δs and change of unit tangent ΔT, consider |ΔT|/Δs and its limit as the step shrinks. Parametrize a smooth curve by arc length s; its unit tangent T(s) is the direction of travel, of length 1. Curvature magnitude is κ(s)=|dT/ds|, where the derivative denotes that rate of change. Around a circle, the tangent turns through angle 2π over arc length 2πR, giving 1/R.

It halves. At R=1 the direction turns by 1 radian (about 57 degrees); at R=2 by 0.5 radian; at R=3 by about 0.33 radian. Over a fixed walk the turn is s/R, inversely proportional to the radius, and “curvature = 1/R” is just that inverse proportionality packed into one number. However large the radius, the turn never reaches zero, but it gets as close as you like. A straight line can be seen as where circles end up as their radius grows without bound, and it has curvature 0.

Here κ=|dT/ds| is a magnitude, so it cannot be negative. A signed curvature can be defined by choosing a normal and calling bending toward that normal positive. Bending the other way is negative. If, say, you take the normal on the left of the direction of travel, left-hand bends are positive and right-hand bends negative. This describes bending relative to the chosen normal: pick the opposite normal and the signs swap.

So what is negative in “negative curvature”? As long as we look only at curves, the sign is settled by a convention about which side counts as positive. On surfaces, a sign appears that survives any change of that convention.

2 / Surfaces in three dimensions

A surface has two principal ways to bend

From a point on a surface you can set off in any direction, and the bending can differ from direction to direction. The point of this section is that all those directions are described by just two numbers.

Start with shapes. The surface of a globe bends to the same side, toward the center of the Earth, whichever way you walk. The middle of a horse saddle is different. Looking front to back along the horse's spine, the front and back rise up: a bowl. Looking side to side across the spine, both flanks hang down: a hill. The edge of a potato chip has the same shape. A rolled-up sheet of paper, a cylinder, bends around the tube but is straight along its axis.

To turn bending in each direction into a number, magnify a neighborhood of one point.

  1. Tangent plane: the plane touching the surface at that point with the same slope. Under magnification, a smooth surface approaches this plane.
  2. Normal: a unit vector perpendicular to the tangent plane.
  3. Section: choose a tangent direction and cut the surface by the plane spanned by that direction and the normal. The cut is a plane curve. On the saddle, it is the cut made by slicing vertically along the spine.
  4. Normal curvature: how much that section bends toward the normal is the normal curvature in that direction. It is the signed curvature of Section 1, measured against the normal.

Choose one side's unit normal; for the paraboloids below it points upward. As the tangent direction turns, normal curvature varies. Two perpendicular directions attaining its maximum and minimum are principal directions, and their values are k₁,k₂. Reversing the normal reverses both signs but leaves their product K=k₁k₂ unchanged. These two numbers give the first local description of shape.

To see why these directions exist, align the tangent plane with the xy plane and write the surface locally as z=f(x,y). Choose coordinates so height and slope vanish at the point. With our sign convention, the second derivative in a direction gives its normal curvature there: it measures change of slope. For z=x²−y² at the origin, the x direction bends upward and the y direction downward. The second-derivative matrix [[fxx,fxy],[fxy,fyy]] is symmetric, since smooth functions satisfy fxy=fyx. Linear algebra diagonalizes a symmetric matrix in perpendicular axes. Those axes are the principal directions and the diagonal entries are k₁,k₂.

Two examples. For z=x²−y², fxx=2, fyy=−2, fxy=0: the matrix is already diagonal, the principal directions are the x and y axes, and k₁=2, k₂=−2. For z=xy, though, fxx=fyy=0, fxy=1. Along the x and y axes the height stays 0, and no bending is visible at all. Diagonalizing [[0,1],[1,0]] gives eigenvalues ±1 with eigenvectors along the two diagonals. Indeed, rotating coordinates by 45° with u=(x+y)/√2, w=(x−y)/√2 gives xy=(u²−w²)/2. So z=xy is a saddle turned by 45°, with k₁=1, k₂=−1. The coordinate axes need not be principal directions, which is why we diagonalize.

At the center of z=(x²−y²)/2, for example, k₁=1,k₂=-1, hence K=-1. A surface is two-dimensional, so this negative sectional curvature is one number for its whole tangent plane. It is not negative sectional curvature in only one direction. The cylinder-type case k₁=1,k₂=0 looks bent but has K=0.

Taking stock: what exactly is negative?
  • Curve curvature magnitude κ is nonnegative. Signed curvature has a sign relative to a chosen normal.
  • Surface normal curvature k(v) in one direction also has a sign relative to a chosen surface normal; reversing the normal flips it.
  • The sign of Gaussian curvature K=k₁k₂ does not depend on the choice of normal. On a surface, it is the sectional curvature attached to the whole tangent plane. Sectional curvature belongs to a two-dimensional plane in the tangent space, not to a single direction alone.

3 / Distances within the surface

A cylinder looks curved but has zero Gaussian curvature

There is a reason to pause here. When we study spaces of dimension three and higher, we have no “outside” from which to look at them. So we need to recast curvature as something an inhabitant of the space can measure from within.

Viewed in three-dimensional space, a cylinder bends. But rolling a sheet of paper into a cylinder leaves lengths and angles measured on the paper unchanged. Cutting it lengthwise and unrolling restores a locally flat sheet. An orange peel, by contrast, cannot be flattened without tearing or stretching it. That is also why every world map distorts distances or areas somewhere.

Gaussian curvature does not simply count bending seen by an outside observer. It detects curvature a tiny surveyor living on the surface can find by measuring distances along it. A cylinder has no local distortion of these distances, hence zero Gaussian curvature.

Curves are the extreme case. Picture an ant living on a wire: all it can measure is distance traveled. However the wire is bent, a short stretch of it matches a straight segment with lengths preserved, so the ant has no way to detect the bending. Indeed, however a one-dimensional curve is drawn in space, its own distance gives zero Riemannian curvature, and it has no two-dimensional plane on which to define sectional curvature. Curvature measurable from within first appears in dimension two.

Another test is a small triangle whose sides are shortest surface paths. For a geodesic triangle, the angle sum minus its flat value is the integral of curvature over its interior. Area integration divides the triangle into tiny pieces and adds curvature times area; dA denotes area. Angles use radians, with π radians equal to 180°.

Angle sum − π = ∫triangle K dA

Positive Gaussian curvature gives a small triangle angle sum greater than π; negative curvature gives less. A small cylinder triangle becomes a plane triangle when unrolled.

Let us check this once on a large triangle. On the unit sphere (where K=1 everywhere), join the North Pole, the point on the equator at longitude 0°, and the point on the equator at longitude 90°, using two meridians and the equator. Meridians meet the equator at right angles, and the two meridians meet at the pole at 90°, so all three angles are right angles. The angle sum is 3π/2, exceeding π by π/2. The triangle is one eighth of the sphere, so its area is 4π/8=π/2. Indeed, angle sum − π = K × area = π/2. On flat paper, no triangle has three right angles.

The distinction: Principal curvatures k₁,k₂ depend on embedding in ambient three-dimensional space. Their product K is determined by distances within the surface. Looking bent and being intrinsically curved are different properties.

4 / From a surface sum to higher dimensions

One term on a surface, two terms in three dimensions

Now the main event. In dimensions three and higher, a point has infinitely many two-dimensional “planes of directions,” and each carries a number playing the role of a surface's Gaussian curvature. That number is sectional curvature. Ricci curvature fixes one direction of travel and collects the sectional curvatures of the planes containing it. First consider surfaces, where there is only one plane.

On a surface the tangent space is already a two-dimensional plane. For unit v, there is just one perpendicular unit direction e, up to sign. Together they span the whole tangent plane, whose sectional curvature is Gaussian curvature K. The Ricci sum has one term: Ric(v,v)=K.

We mean Ric(v,v) for a unit direction v. Starting with surfaces makes “sum of sectional curvatures” begin with one term on a surface; higher dimension adds transverse planes.

In dimensions three and above, more tangent directions leave a point p. Choose a unit direction v of travel. The curvature of each plane containing it is sectional curvature.

Here is a way to picture the curvature of a single plane. From p, send out short geodesics (the paths that go straight within the space) in every direction lying in the chosen plane σ, and only those. Together they sweep out a small surface inside the space. The Gaussian curvature of that surface at p is the sectional curvature K(σ) of the plane. Read it as how a thin “section” of the space, cut in that direction, is curved, and the name sectional curvature fits. Section 5 gives the precise formula.

In three dimensions the perpendicular complement of v is a plane. Choose perpendicular unit basis vectors e₁,e₂, and examine the curvatures of the planes spanned by v,e₁ and v,e₂.

Ricci curvature does not keep the details of each section, only the transverse total seen from the chosen direction of travel. Adding things up loses information, and that is the character of Ricci curvature.

The lower row shows values that occur in actual three-dimensional spaces. On the unit three-sphere S³ every plane has sectional curvature 1, so Ric=2; on hyperbolic space H³ (sectional curvature −1 everywhere), Ric=−2. S²×ℝ is the product of the unit sphere S² and a line ℝ; think of copies of the sphere stacked endlessly in a vertical direction. Travel in a direction v along the sphere, and the two transverse planes are “the sphere itself” (sectional curvature 1) and “the plane spanned by v and the vertical” (sectional curvature 0), so Ric=1. Travel vertically along ℝ, and both transverse planes contain the vertical direction and are flat, so Ric=0. At the same point, Ricci curvature changes with the direction of travel. That is why we write Ric(v,v) as a function of the direction v.

In n dimensions, the perpendicular complement of unit v has dimension n−1. Choose perpendicular unit vectors e₂,…,eₙ there and add the curvatures of their planes with v:

Ric(v,v) = K(v,e₂)+K(v,e₃)+⋯+K(v,eₙ),　|v|=1.

What is “counted” is the sum of sectional curvatures over all perpendicular transverse directions, rather than a number of points or curves. Dividing by n−1 gives mean sectional curvature. The usual Ricci tensor uses the sum, not that mean.

5 / From diagrams to definitions

Define the sectional-curvature sum as a trace

Why are the definitions so involved? On a surface we could raise a normal into the surrounding three-dimensional space and measure bending toward it. A general space has no outside. So we measure bending again using operations available inside the space: carry a vector along without turning it, and notice that changing the route changes the result.

For example, stand at the North Pole of the unit sphere holding a spear level, pointing south. Never turn the spear yourself. Walk down the meridian at longitude 0° to the equator; the spear keeps pointing south, straight ahead. At the equator turn east and walk along it to longitude 90°; the spear still points south, now off to your right. Then walk back up the meridian to the pole; the spear points south, which is now behind you. Compare at the pole: the spear started out pointing along the meridian at longitude 0° and comes back pointing along the meridian at longitude 90°. It has turned by 90°. That 90° (π/2) is the same as the angle excess of the triangle in Section 3, the enclosed area times the curvature. On flat ground, the spear returns to its original direction along any closed route.

The curvature tensor measures this “a round trip turns the vector” effect on infinitesimally small loops. The terminology below describes stages of the same construction: choose a plane, measure its curvature, and compare it with the other planes.

A Riemannian manifold is a smooth space locally described by n real coordinates, with an inner product g smoothly specifying lengths and angles at each point. The vector space of possible directions at p is the tangent space. Its inner product gives lengths |X|=√g(X,X) and orthogonality.

To turn the spear story into formulas we need three tools, introduced in order. Vector fields X,Y,Z assign a vector to each point.

  1. Directional derivative of a function, X(h): acting on a function h, a field X gives the rate of change of h when moving in the direction of X. If X points right in the plane and h=x is horizontal position, X(h)=1.
  2. The commutator [X,Y]: defined on functions by [X,Y](h)=X(Y(h))−Y(X(h)), it compares looking at change first along Y and then along X with the reverse order. For example, if X points right and Y points north with magnitude proportional to horizontal position x, moving right increases the northward field and [X,Y] points north. Coordinate fields in the same chart, such as motions along perpendicular coordinate axes, satisfy [X,Y]=0. This quantity measures how the direction fields themselves are arranged, not how the space bends.
  3. Differentiating a vector field (a connection), ∇XY: in the plane, differentiating Y along X examines how it changes after moving slightly. On a surface, discard the component normal to the surface. The corresponding operation on a general space, preserving lengths and angles, is a connection, written ∇XY. Carrying the spear “without turning it” means carrying it so that this derivative along the route vanishes (parallel transport). We impose two conditions.
    • Compatibility with the inner product: X(g(Y,Z))=g(∇XY,Z)+g(Y,∇XZ). Transport does not change lengths or angles.
    • Torsion-free: ∇XY−∇YX=[X,Y], so the difference of these derivatives agrees with the commutator.
    Exactly one connection satisfies both conditions, the Levi-Civita connection.

The curvature tensor compares derivatives of Z in the orders X,Y and Y,X. The directions themselves may vary, requiring the final correction ∇[X,Y]Z. Subtracting it removes changes of the direction fields themselves and leaves only the bending of the space. We choose the convention making sphere curvature positive:

R(X,Y)Z = ∇X∇YZ − ∇Y∇XZ − ∇[X,Y]Z.

In flat space ℝⁿ, R=0. The curvature tensor records how far a vector, parallel-transported around an infinitesimal loop, has rotated when it returns. On a surface, the spear's 90° equals ∫K dA over the region the loop encloses, the same value as the triangle formula of Section 3.

Sectional curvature

Let perpendicular unit vectors u,w span a two-dimensional plane σ in the tangent space at p. Define

K(σ) = K(u,w) = g(R(u,w)w,u).

For arbitrary independent u,w, divide the right side by g(u,u)g(w,w)−g(u,w)², the squared area of their parallelogram. The result depends only on σ, not on its basis.

Ricci curvature (Ricci tensor)

For an orthonormal tangent basis e₁,…,eₙ at p, define

Ric(X,Y) = Σi=1n g(R(eᵢ,X)Y,eᵢ).

For unit v choose e₁=v. Curvature symmetries give g(R(eᵢ,v)v,eᵢ)=g(R(v,eᵢ)eᵢ,v), whose right side is the defining sectional curvature. Therefore

Ric(v,v) = Σi=2n K(v,eᵢ).

Equal first two inputs make the curvature tensor vanish, so the travel direction's own term is zero. The remaining terms are the planes paired with perpendicular directions.

Why does a different orthonormal basis give the same answer? Matrix trace, the sum of diagonal entries, is unchanged by orthonormal basis changes. Ricci takes the trace of a curvature operator, so any perpendicular basis gives the same sum. For a two-by-two matrix, trace is a+d. This combines many sectional curvatures into one value for each direction.

Check the sign on a sphere

A standard sphere of radius a has sectional curvature K=1/a² on every tangent two-plane. In dimension n,

Ric(v,v) = (n−1)/a²　(|v|=1).

For a three-dimensional sphere of radius 2, every sectional curvature is 1/4. Two transverse directions give Ricci curvature 1/4+1/4=1/2. Our convention is positive on spheres.

Write the trace in linear algebra notation

Fix X and consider A_X:Z↦R(Z,X)X. For unit X, Ric(X,X)=tr(A_X). Taking trace in an orthonormal basis yields exactly the sectional-curvature sum. Its symmetric bilinear extension to arbitrary X,Y is the Ricci tensor defined above.

6 / Why a transverse sum?

The sum appears in the volume of a geodesic bundle

This section answers the opening question: why a sum? The one-line answer first. Volume is a product of stretchings in each direction, and a product of small corrections is, to first order, their sum. Shrink a rectangle by 1% in height and 2% in width, and its area becomes 0.99×0.98=0.9702 times as large: almost exactly 3% smaller. The product of the corrections, 0.01×0.02, is much smaller and drops out at first order. What remains is to see that each direction's stretching is governed by a sectional curvature.

Send geodesics from p at equal speeds in slightly different directions. A geodesic generalizes a locally shortest surface path; the meridians leaving the North Pole in the introduction are examples. The displacement of neighboring paths from a reference path is described by a vector field called a Jacobi field.

First in two dimensions, fix the initial angular spread and distance traveled. Measure transverse width along the geodesic circle of radius r between two paths starting at the same point. In the plane it grows proportionally to r; curvature changes that rate.

Here j(r) is width per unit initial angle. An angular spread Δθ has arc length j(r)Δθ along the constant-curvature geodesic circle. This is not the shortest distance between its endpoints.

The three functions are exact solutions of the two-dimensional constant-curvature Jacobi equation j''+Kj=0, with j(0)=0 and j'(0)=1. The second derivative measures change of spreading speed. For positive curvature j''=−j slows it; for negative curvature j''=j accelerates it. Even positive curvature gives growing width for small r, not immediate contraction of all distances. This illustrates the local mechanism and does not prove finite-distance behavior on a general manifold. See Ohta, §2, equations (2.1)–(2.2) and §3.1, equation (3.1).

For a unit-speed reference geodesic γ(r) and transverse Jacobi field J(r),

Dr²J + R(J,γ′)γ′ = 0,　J(0)=0.

Dr is covariant differentiation along the geodesic. Once initial spreading DrJ(0) is specified, curvature determines its evolution. One transverse direction made this vector equation a scalar width equation in the experiment.

Higher dimension has more transverse directions and a transverse volume. As an analogy, think of a narrow flashlight beam leaving a point. In flat space the illuminated cross-section grows like distance to the power n−1. In a curved space, each transverse direction spreads in its own way, as in the fan above. Multiplying their spreads makes the first curvature correction a sum of sectional curvatures, for the same reason as in the rectangle computation: Ric(v,v)=ΣK(v,eᵢ).

For volume comparison, consider a tiny directional neighborhood of unit v, then let its angular width tend to zero and measure cross-sectional volume per unit angular size at distance r. Divide this density by the flat density rn−1. For a bundle of finite angular width, the first r² correction averages Ricci curvature over its directions; higher corrections are not determined by that average alone. Here shrinking angular width selects the central direction v:

Volume density ratio = 1 − Ric(v,v) r²/6 + O(r³)　(r→0).

This is the local geodesic-polar expansion from p in direction v. The remainder O(r³) has absolute value bounded by a constant times r³ for sufficiently small r. It gives a quadratic change near the origin, not complete finite-distance volume from one Ricci value. If Ric(v,v)>0, small-distance bundle volume begins smaller than in flat space.

Check this on the unit three-sphere S³. There are two transverse directions, each with the same width sin r as in the fan, so the volume density ratio is (sin r / r)². Squaring sin r / r ≈ 1 − r²/6 gives 1 − 2·r²/6 = 1 − r²/3, dropping terms of order r⁴ and higher. On S³, Ric(v,v)=2, so the formula's right side is also 1 − 2r²/6 = 1 − r²/3. Each of the two transverse directions contributes one correction −r²/6, and their sum is where the 2 comes from.

Derive the coefficient 1/6 and the Ricci sum from products of widths

First follow one transverse displacement on a surface. Vary the initial direction of a reference geodesic and travel the same small distance r. Endpoints lie on a small geodesic circle centered at p. Their variation is tangent to the circle, while γ′(r) is radial. Gauss's lemma gives their orthogonality. More concretely, the derivative of distance in direction V is g(V,γ′(r)), the radial component. Motion on the circle leaves distance unchanged, so that component is zero. The Jacobi field J(r) is thus perpendicular to γ′(r). On a surface there is only one such tangent direction.

Parallel-transport a unit vector E(r) in this perpendicular direction. Write J(r)=j(r)E(r), with j(r) the signed displacement for unit initial angular variation. The curvature operator R(E,γ′)γ′ is perpendicular with coefficient g(R(E,γ′)γ′,E)=K(r), the surface's Gaussian curvature. In this parallel frame the Jacobi equation becomes the ordinary differential equation j''(r)+K(r)j(r)=0.

All paths meet initially, so j(0)=0; normalize j'(0)=1. Substitution gives j''(0)=0. Differentiating, j'''(r)+K'(r)j(r)+K(r)j'(r)=0, by the product rule (Kj)'=K'j+Kj'. Thus j'''(0)=−K(0). Successive derivatives describe the rate of change and its subsequent changes.

A Taylor expansion approximates a smooth function near a point by a polynomial matching its derivatives. For a₀+a₁r+a₂r²+a₃r³, the value and first three derivatives at zero are a₀,a₁,2a₂,6a₃. Hence the cubic approximation is j(0)+j'(0)r+j''(0)r²/2+j'''(0)r³/6. For example, differentiating 5r³ three times gives 5×3×2×1=30, recovering its coefficient as 30/6=5.

Substitution gives j(r)=r−K(0)r³/6+O(r⁴), where the remainder is bounded by a constant times r⁴. Relative to flat spread r, j(r)/r=1−K(0)r²/6+O(r³). In higher dimension, corresponding transverse corrections multiply to give volume.

Bundle Jacobi fields into a matrix; the determinant gives small transverse volume. The transverse curvature operator is symmetric, so choose at p an orthonormal eigenbasis e₁,…,eₙ₋₁ and transport it parallelly. The second-order length correction for axis i is −Kᵢr²/6, with Kᵢ=K(v,eᵢ) at the starting point. The operator need not remain diagonal along the path, but the starting operator determines this order of Taylor correction. Thus the product (1−K₁r²/6)(1−K₂r²/6)⋯ contributes −(K₁+⋯+Kₙ₋₁)r²/6. The sum is the curvature operator's trace, equal to Ric(v,v). Ricci therefore governs the first curvature correction to transverse volume around one geodesic.

The opening question answered: Ricci sums sectional curvatures over perpendicular transverse directions. Their total describes how nearby geodesics' cross-sectional volume first deviates from flat behavior.

7 / From pointwise curvature to a uniform condition

A Ricci lower bound is a floor at every point and in every direction

So far everything has been about one point and one direction. To say something about the whole space, such as how large its balls can be, we need an assumption usable everywhere and in every direction. So we put a floor under Ricci curvature.

If Ricci curvature never drops below a constant K₀, as point p and unit direction v vary, this is a Ricci lower bound: Ricp(v,v)≥K₀. For arbitrary tangent vectors it reads

Ricp(X,X) ≥ K₀ |X|²　(for every point p and tangent vector X).

This is a uniform minimum, not positivity at one location. A two-dimensional sphere of radius a has Ric(v,v)=1/a², so any K₀≤1/a² is a lower bound. A plane or cylinder has Ric=0. If a saddle center has Ric=-1, no bound K₀>-1 can hold on an entire space containing it.

It is also worth seeing a case where the value depends on direction. On S²×ℝ from Section 4 (the unit sphere times a line), split a tangent vector into its component XS along the sphere and its vertical component Xℝ. Then Ric(X,X)=|XS|². This is nonnegative, so K₀=0 is a lower bound. In the vertical direction Ric=0, so no larger bound is possible. Along the sphere Ric=1, but the floor is set by the lowest direction.

This differs from bounding every sectional curvature below.

If every two-plane has sectional curvature at least κ, summing n−1 terms yields Ric≥(n−1)κg. The converse generally fails. A sum can stay positive despite negative sections if others compensate. Try −2+3=1 in the three-dimensional sliders.

A Ricci bound in comparison geometry concerns more than the appearance of one surface point. As points and travel directions vary, it uniformly restricts how much geodesic bundles and balls can expand.

Rephrase a Ricci bound through transport of measures

A Ricci lower bound controls volume change in geodesic families. Write that change as an entropy inequality along optimal transport, and the CD condition extends the idea to spaces without a differentiable structure. Add a Hilbertian energy condition and one obtains RCD. The definitions and proof calculations are developed in CD and RCD Conditions from Ricci Curvature.

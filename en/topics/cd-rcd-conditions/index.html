Topics / Geometry / Ricci curvature without coordinates

CD and RCD Conditions from Ricci Curvature

On a smooth manifold, Ricci curvature is a tensor on each tangent space. But spaces that arise as limits of manifolds may have no tangent spaces and no derivatives at all. So we want to say “Ricci curvature is bounded below” without differentiating. The key picture is simple. Carry a cloud of mass along shortest paths: in positively curved space the cloud bulges on the way, and in negatively curved space it pinches. A bulging cloud thins out, and its entropy drops. The CD condition turns “how high the entropy may rise during transport” into the definition of curvature. This page first lets you see the effect in experiments, then states the definition, follows the smooth calculation in which a Ricci lower bound produces it, and finally identifies what the letter R in RCD adds.

Requiring this bound on general spaces leads to the definition of CD(K,∞). For the picture of a cloud bulging in curved space, see ③; for the calculation showing why smooth Ricci curvature enters, follow the second derivative of the Jacobian.

02 / Norm → Energy

What does the R add to CD?

The CD condition only sees how the density of a cloud changes under transport. So CD can hold even when the unit ball of the ruler, the norm, is not round. The R in RCD demands, in the language of energy, that the ruler come from an inner product: that its unit ball be a circle (or an ellipse, in other coordinates). In this experiment you deform the unit ball of the plane from a circle into a diamond or a square and watch what breaks.

This tests a local energy density in a normed plane, not the full Cheeger energy of an affine function on infinite space. Non-Euclidean normed CD examples exist. For p≠2 in this family, however, the parallelogram identity fails, excluding the additional Hilbertian condition in RCD.

Why does energy use the dual norm?

The largest absolute directional derivative of a function f (with differential ξ=df) over unit tangent vectors is sup‖v‖p≤1|ξ(v)|=‖ξ‖q. Thus the density corresponding to gradient squared is E(ξ)=½‖ξ‖q². For finite exponents, ‖ξ‖q=(|ξ₁|ᑫ+|ξ₂|ᑫ)1/q; for q=∞ use the maximum.

Test the covectors u=(1,0), v=(0,1). We have E(u)=E(v)=½ and E(u+v)=E(u−v)=½·22/q, so

Δ=E(u+v)+E(u−v)−2E(u)−2E(v)=22/q−2

This vanishes exactly at p=2, or q=2. On a general space RCD requires quadraticity of Cheeger energy for all finite-energy functions, beyond an equality for one test pair.

Why define curvature using only distance and measure?

A Ricci lower bound controls the transverse volume expansion of geodesic families. If the metric and volume measure converge, we would like that bound to survive. But a limit need not be a smooth manifold, so tangent spaces and curvature tensors may no longer exist.

Instead, consider a triple (X,d,m): a set X, a distance d, and a reference measure m, which is Riemannian volume in the smooth case. A CD condition uses only these data and agrees with smooth Ricci bounds. Because it is formulated in terms of transport and measures, it can also describe singular limits.

The idea is the same as the definition of a convex function. The condition f″≥0 uses a second derivative, but rephrased as “the graph lies below its chords” it needs no derivatives at all. There is also a quantitative version that plays the role of a curvature lower bound. For a C² function f on the line, f″≥K holds if and only if, for all x,y and t∈[0,1],

f((1−t)x+ty) ≤ (1−t)f(x)+t f(y) − (K/2)t(1−t)|x−y|²

(rewrite it as convexity of f−Kx²/2; equality holds for f=Kx²/2). The CD condition replaces f by entropy, the points x,y by probability measures, and |x−y| by the Wasserstein distance. Instead of looking at the Ricci tensor directly, it requires, through a chord comparison, that entropy be “curved by at least K” on the space of measures.

We first study CD(K,∞), which does not impose a finite dimension bound. We then see how finite N enters CD(K,N), and distinguish the Riemannian condition RCD.

The picture first: curvature makes transported clouds bulge

Why should curvature have anything to do with entropy? Before the formulas, here is the argument as a picture.

Take a small cloud at the start and a cloud of the same size at the destination, and carry each point of the cloud along a shortest path at constant speed. In flat space, a cloud carried in parallel keeps its size the whole way. In positively curved space, a bundle of shortest paths first spreads and then gathers again: carry a cloud along meridians on a sphere from near the north pole to near the south pole, and the bundle is thickest at the equator. So the cloud bulges in the middle. In negatively curved space it is the other way round: a bundle whose two ends have matching sizes pinches in the middle. In the optimal transport literature this is known as the “lazy gas experiment.”

From this picture to the CD condition is three steps.

  1. Curvature → volume. If Ric≥K>0, the intermediate volume is larger compared with the volumes at the ends (for K<0 it is allowed to be smaller). Precisely, the logarithm of the volume ratio Jₜ lies above its endpoint chord by at least (K/2)t(1−t)L².
  2. Volume → density. Mass is conserved, so where the volume has grown the density has dropped. In formulas, ρₜ(Fₜ(x))Jₜ(x)=ρ₀(x).
  3. Density → entropy. A thinner cloud has smaller entropy. So the intermediate entropy falls below the chord joining its endpoint values. That is the inequality of the CD condition.

Let us check this once with numbers. On a surface of constant curvature κ, the size j of a transverse Jacobi field along a geodesic of speed L satisfies j″+κL²j=0. With ω=√|κ|·L, the solution with j(0)=j(1)=1 has middle value j(½)=1/cos(ω/2) for κ>0 (with ω<π) and j(½)=1/cosh(ω/2) for κ<0. For the figure’s ω=1.6 these are about 1.44 and about 0.75. In both cases log j(½) ≥ κL²/8. On a surface Ric(γ̇,γ̇)=κL², so this is exactly the step-one comparison with t=½ and K=κ.

On a weighted space, the weight of the reference measure can play the same role even without any curvature. The line of experiment ① is an example: for K>0 the weight e−Kx²/2 counts the region near the origin heavily, so a cloud passing through it looks larger as measured by mK, and thinner. The next two sections set up the language of measures, and the smooth section checks steps one to three by calculation.

From point geodesics to measure geodesics

The CD condition compares entropies “along the way” as a cloud is carried. So we first need the cheapest way to carry the cloud itself, and what it looks like in between.

Start with a small example. On the real line, let μ₀ put mass ½ at each of the points 0 and 1, and let μ₁ put mass ½ at each of 2 and 3. Carrying them in order, 0→2 and 1→3, gives average squared distance ½(2²+2²)=4. Crossing them, 0→3 and 1→2, gives ½(3²+1²)=5. Every other plan mixes these two, with cost between 4 and 5. So the order-preserving plan is optimal and W₂²(μ₀,μ₁)=4. Moving each mass point along its shortest path to time t=½ gives the intermediate cloud ½δ₁+½δ₂. The “matching quantiles” of experiment ① is the continuous version of this order-preserving plan.

In general, the definitions are as follows. Think of probability measures μ₀,μ₁ as two clouds of mass. A coupling π records how much mass travels from each starting point x to each endpoint y; its marginals are μ₀,μ₁. Minimizing squared transport cost defines the quadratic Wasserstein distance:

π∈P(X×X),　(p₀)#π=μ₀,　(p₁)#π=μ₁
W₂²(μ₀,μ₁)= infπ with these marginals ∫ d(x,y)² dπ(x,y).

Here p₀(x,y)=x and p₁(x,y)=y are the coordinate projections. For a measurable map T, T#μ denotes the pushforward measure, defined by (T#μ)(A)=μ(T⁻¹(A)); thus (p₀)#π=μ₀ says that the starting-point distribution of π is μ₀.

P(X) denotes probability measures on X, and P₂(X) consists of those μ for which ∫d(x*,x)²dμ(x)<∞ for one (hence every) base point x*. This condition is independent of the base point by the triangle inequality. Given an optimal coupling, move its mass along minimizing constant-speed geodesics joining the paired endpoints. A probability measure Π on the path space records this family; if eₜ(γ)=γ(t) evaluates a path at time t, the intermediate measure is μₜ=(eₜ)#Π. The curve t↦μₜ is a geodesic for W₂, called displacement interpolation.

Write Geo(X) for constant-speed minimizing geodesics in X. An optimal coupling of endpoint pairs is not the same object as a path measure Π∈P(Geo(X)): the former records endpoint pairs, the latter records whole paths. On general spaces, neither optimal couplings nor geodesics need be unique. The CD definition therefore includes the existence of at least one suitable path measure.

CD(K,∞): entropy is convex with a curvature correction

Why entropy? Because we want a single number for how tightly packed a cloud is. For example, the uniform distribution on an interval of length ℓ in the real line has density 1/ℓ with respect to Lebesgue measure, and the definition below gives Ent=−log ℓ. Doubling the width lowers the entropy by log 2. Spread out means small, concentrated means large. This number is how we read the “bulge and thin out” of the picture.

If μ=ρm, define relative entropy by

Entm(μ)=∫ ρ log ρ dm.

We call a measure finite-entropy when this integral is a finite real number. Assign entropy +∞ to every measure that is not absolutely continuous with respect to m (including measures with both absolutely continuous and singular parts), or when the integral is undefined. This makes the inequality meaningful even if an intermediate measure is singular or mixed. Because r↦r log r is convex, concentrating density tends to increase this quantity. This sign convention is the negative of the usual Boltzmann entropy.

Definition (entropic CD(K,∞), restricted here to absolutely continuous endpoints). Let X be a complete, separable geodesic metric space and let m be a Borel measure that is positive and finite on every ball. For every pair of absolutely continuous finite-entropy measures μ₀,μ₁∈P₂(X), there is at least one W₂-geodesic (μₜ)0≤t≤1 such that, for every t∈[0,1],

Entm(μt) ≤ (1−t)Entm(μ₀)+t Entm(μ₁) − (K/2)t(1−t)W₂²(μ₀,μ₁).

This readable form restricts the endpoints to be absolutely continuous; standard metric-measure definitions extend the functionals to singular parts. Some stronger variants require the inequality along every suitable transport geodesic. Do not silently replace the existential quantifier above by that stronger condition.

The shape is that of the chord comparison for f″≥K in the first section, with entropy in place of f and W₂ in place of |x−y|. If K>0, the intermediate entropy must lie below the linear interpolation by a larger amount; if K<0, the permitted upper bound rises. Thus the squared distance between distributions quantifies the curvature correction to convexity. On the weighted line of experiment ①, translation at equal width makes this inequality an exact equality, and transports that change the width leave room to spare.

On a complete smooth Riemannian manifold with m=volg, this condition corresponds to Ric≥Kg. For a weighted measure m=e^{-ψ}volg, the corresponding tensor is Ric+Hess ψ, the infinite-dimensional Bakry–Émery tensor.

Smooth case: Ricci lower bound implies entropy convexity

This section turns the three steps of the picture (curvature → volume → density → entropy) into a calculation. The proof converts curvature into a pointwise volume estimate, uses mass conservation to convert volume into density, then integrates to obtain entropy. We first calculate along a regular geodesic family. A separate optimal-transport regularity theorem ensures the formulas apply for almost every starting point.

Analytic input. On a complete smooth boundaryless Riemannian manifold, compactly supported endpoint measures absolutely continuous with respect to volume and of finite entropy admit an optimal map for the squared-distance cost; its geodesic interpolation is a W₂-geodesic. Differentiability and change of variables hold almost everywhere with respect to the source measure μ₀, in particular where 0<ρ₀<∞. The nondegeneracy theorem for optimal transport, using absolute continuity of both endpoints, also gives Jₜ>0 on the full interval [0,1] for almost every relevant optimal geodesic. This lets us compare log Jₜ all the way to the endpoints. Differentiability and this nondegeneracy are optimal-transport and area-formula results, not consequences of the curvature calculation alone.

The function φ comes from an optimal-transport potential. Optimality for the squared-distance cost implies that, at regular starting points, the velocity is the gradient of a potential; absorbing the sign into φ, write F₁(x)=expx(∇φ(x)). We use this representation as an optimal-transport theorem. Even in the smooth case it need not hold at every point; it holds almost everywhere away from cut-locus and other singular points.

A rehearsal in one dimension. On the line of experiment ①, the optimal map is Tₜ(x)=aₜ+(σₜ/σ₀)(x−a₀), and its derivative Jₜ=σₜ/σ₀ was the particle spacing ratio. Since Jₜ is linear in t, log Jₜ is concave and log Jₜ ≥ (1−t)log J₀+t log J₁. The line is not curved, so this is the K=0 case of the Jacobian comparison below (the experiment’s K enters separately as the weight’s Hess ψ). What follows computes, with Jacobi fields, how curvature adds to this concavity of log Jₜ in a curved space.

1. Differentiate the optimal map by Jacobi fields

Use this optimal map as F₁ and set Fₜ(x)=expx(t∇φ(x)). This moves each mass point at constant speed along a minimizing geodesic. Varying x with velocity v gives a variation field J(t)=D Fₜ(x)v, which satisfies the Jacobi equation

Dₜ²J + R(J,γ̇)γ̇=0.

Here Dₜ is covariant differentiation along the geodesic and R is the curvature tensor. Put the n independent variation fields from moving the starting point into a matrix Aₜ=D Fₜ(x), using a frame parallel along the geodesic. Its determinant Jₜ(x)=det Aₜ is the volume multiplier. We use Jₜ for this determinant to distinguish it from an individual Jacobi field.

2. Ricci appears in the second derivative of the full transport Jacobian

Jₜ=det Aₜ measures change in the full n-dimensional volume of the mass cloud, not just a cross-section perpendicular to the geodesic. On an interval where the geodesic family has not collapsed, set Bₜ=A′ₜAₜ⁻¹. Identify tangent spaces by parallel transport along the geodesic. The matrix Jacobi equation yields the Riccati equation B′+B²+Rγ̇=0. Using (log det A)′=tr(A′A⁻¹), we get

(log Jₜ)′ = tr Bₜ,
(log Jₜ)″ = −tr(Bₜ²)−Ric(γ̇,γ̇).

Here is how to read these formulas. tr Bₜ is the expansion rate of the cloud’s volume. The second line says how that rate decreases over time. The term −tr(Bₜ²), present for every bundle, always pushes the expansion rate down, and −Ric(γ̇,γ̇) is the extra push from curvature. The larger Ric is, the faster the expansion rate falls, and the more strongly concave log Jₜ becomes. A concave function bulges above its chord, so this is the formula behind the picture of a cloud bulging on the way (it has the same form as what relativity calls the Raychaudhuri equation). The next paragraphs make this precise.

At time t, define the velocity field vₜ=∂ₜFₜ∘Fₜ⁻¹. Optimality implies that on a regular region it is a gradient ∇ψₜ of a time-dependent potential (another transport theorem). In a parallel frame, the chain rule gives Dvₜ(Fₜ(x))=A′ₜAₜ⁻¹=Bₜ, the symmetric Hessian of ψₜ. Hence tr(Bₜ²)≥0. Taking the trace of the Riccati equation gives (tr B)′+tr(B²)+Ric(γ̇,γ̇)=0. Since the speed L=|γ̇| is constant and Ric≥Kg,

u″(t)≤−K L²,　u(t):=log Jₜ.

Subtract the endpoint chord and curvature term: q(t)=u(t)−(1−t)u(0)−tu(1)−(K/2)L²t(1−t). Then q(0)=q(1)=0 and q″=u″+KL²≤0. A concave function lies above its chord, so q(t)≥0, or

log Jₜ ≥ (1−t)log J₀+t log J₁+(K/2)t(1−t)L².

This is the Jacobian endpoint estimate needed for CD(K,∞). Finite dimension gives sharper distortion coefficients, displayed below.

3. Mass conservation turns Jacobians into densities

For μₜ=ρₜvolg, change of variables gives

ρₜ(Fₜ(x)) Jₜ(x)=ρ₀(x)　(μ₀-almost everywhere, where 0<ρ₀<∞).

Taking logarithms and integrating with dμ₀=ρ₀dvol yields

Ent(μₜ)=Ent(μ₀)−∫log Jₜ(x)dμ₀(x).

Insert the Jacobian comparison; its lower bound becomes an upper bound because of the minus sign: larger volume means smaller density, and smaller entropy. Also, ∫d(x,F₁(x))²dμ₀=W₂²(μ₀,μ₁) precisely because the map is optimal. The result is

Ent(μₜ) ≤ (1−t)Ent(μ₀)+tEnt(μ₁)−(K/2)t(1−t)W₂²(μ₀,μ₁).

Thus a pointwise comparison of the full transport Jacobian becomes an entropy inequality for whole probability clouds. The curvature term is nevertheless transverse: Rγ̇(w)=R(w,γ̇)γ̇ annihilates the direction γ̇. If the geodesic speed is L=|γ̇|, its trace is L² times the sum of sectional curvatures over the n−1 perpendicular directions, namely Ric(γ̇,γ̇) (both sides vanish when L=0). The Jacobian is full-dimensional, while Ricci is the transverse curvature trace controlling its second derivative. The converse uses a separate small-region argument: place uniform measures on small sets and recover the second-order local volume term, hence Ricci curvature.

Regularity and support boundary. The Riccati calculation applies where Fₜ is differentiable and its Jacobian is positive. A general optimal map need not be globally smooth. Measurability, almost-everywhere differentiability, and the area formula are separate inputs. The direct calculation here uses compactly supported endpoints. To reach arbitrary finite-entropy endpoints, truncate to balls and renormalize, apply the compact-support result, take a W₂-convergent subsequence of transport geodesics, and use lower semicontinuity of entropy to pass to the limit; the endpoint entropies converge under this truncation. This approximation and compactness argument is separate from the Jacobi calculation. Ohta's Theorem 4.6 also outlines the proof and invokes optimal-transport regularity.

Finite-dimensional CD(K,N): dimension enters the entropy

Why add dimension? Because CD(K,∞) cannot tell dimensions apart. For example, ℝⁿ with the standard Gaussian measure e−|x|²/2dx satisfies CD(1,∞) for every n (Ric=0, Hess ψ=I). Geometry that depends on dimension, such as comparison of volume growth or diameter bounds, requires recording N in the condition.

CD(K,∞) records a Ricci lower bound but no finite upper dimension. For an unweighted smooth n-manifold the natural threshold is N≥n. Finite N does not mean simply inserting N in the infinite-dimensional formula: it uses Rényi-type entropy and distance-dependent distortion coefficients.

Take N>1 and, for μ=ρm, set SN(μ)=−∫ρ1−1/Ndm. The finite-dimensional transport inequality controls this functional using curvature- and distance-dependent weights along a suitable optimal transport. In the smooth unweighted case, Ric≥K and n≤N correspond to the finite-dimensional CD condition.

The exponent 1−1/N is easiest to understand on a uniform cloud. A cloud spread uniformly over a ball of radius r in N dimensions has density proportional to r−N, so ρ−1/N is proportional to r: a length scale. The pointwise estimate below says, for K=0 (β=1), that this length scale never drops below the linear interpolation of its endpoint values along the transport. It has the same form as the Brunn–Minkowski inequality |(1−t)A+tB|1/n ≥ (1−t)|A|1/n+t|B|1/n for sets in ℝⁿ.

Where dimension comes from is visible in the previous section’s calculation. Unweighted, with Ric≥K, the previous section discarded a term by using tr(Bₜ²)≥0. Since Bₜ is a symmetric n×n matrix, in fact tr(Bₜ²)≥(tr Bₜ)²/n (Cauchy–Schwarz). Keeping it gives Dₜ″≤−(KL²/n)Dₜ for Dₜ=Jₜ1/n. In particular, for K=0, Jₜ1/n is concave, which is the length-scale comparison above. The precise distortion coefficients β below are a sharper form, obtained by treating the direction of motion separately from the directions orthogonal to it.

For a weighted manifold m=e^{-ψ}volg and finite N>n, the relevant tensor is

RicN(v,v)=Ric(v,v)+Hess ψ(v,v)−⟨∇ψ,v⟩²/(N−n).

The denominator N−n is the dimension budget available for the weight. As N→∞, its final term vanishes and we recover Ric+Hess ψ. At the parameter boundary N=n, the formula cannot be used by substitution: the weight must be locally constant on each connected component.

Define the distance factor precisely. Here take N>1, 0<t<1, and r>0. Let sK,N(r) solve s″+(K/(N−1))s=0, s(0)=0, s′(0)=1. For K>0, use it before its first positive zero, π√((N−1)/K). Set βK,Nt(r)=[sK,N(tr)/(t sK,N(r))]N−1. At r=0, set β=1 by continuity; interpret t=0,1 by limits. When K=0, β=1; positive, zero, and negative curvature give sine, linear, and hyperbolic-sine comparison functions respectively.

The following pointwise estimate is a theorem under these hypotheses: as in the preceding section, take a complete smooth weighted n-manifold without boundary, m=e−ψvolg, finite N>n, and RicN≥Kg. Assume compactly supported, absolutely continuous, finite-entropy endpoints and take their optimal map. Then for μ₀-almost every x with 0<ρ₀(x),ρ₁(F₁(x))<∞,

This is the change of variables using μₜ=(Fₜ)#μ₀. Averaging the pointwise density estimate over transported mass gives a Rényi entropy inequality. The curvature-to-distortion comparison itself is invoked above as a smooth theorem; here we work through its integration. On a general metric measure space, the Lott–Villani definition further specifies the whole displacement-convex class of functionals and the treatment of singular parts, which we cite as a theorem. Reduced CD* uses different distortion conventions.

The smooth weighted equivalence says that RicN≥K corresponds to finite-dimensional CD, with N>n using the tensor above and N=n requiring the weight to be locally constant. Ohta's Theorem 4.6 states this equivalence; equations (4.13) and (4.14) lead from mass conservation and the Jacobian estimate to the Rényi inequality (4.11).

RCD adds Hilbertianity of the differential energy

Why add the R? CD only sees how the density of a cloud changes, so it cannot tell whether the ruler for lengths comes from an inner product. The diamond and square norms of experiment ② are examples. When the ruler does come from an inner product, energy behaves like a sum of squares, and heat flow superposes: running heat flow from the sum of two temperature profiles gives the sum of the two separate flows. RCD requires this superposition property, in the form of quadraticity of the Cheeger energy.

The CD condition uses only distance and measure. That flexibility also admits non-Hilbertian geometries, including Finsler examples. R in RCD means Riemannian: an appropriate CD condition is supplemented by the requirement that the energy built from squared gradients is quadratic.

The local slope of f at x is its largest nearby rate of change, lip f(x)=limsupy→x, y≠x|f(y)−f(x)|/d(y,x); set it to zero when x is isolated. Cheeger energy is the lower-semicontinuous relaxation of the squared-slope integral over Lipschitz approximations:

Ch(f)=1/2 inf { liminfj→∞ ∫ (lip fj)² dm : fj∈Lip(X)∩L², fj→f in L² }.

If no such approximating sequence exists, set Ch(f)=+∞. On a smooth Riemannian manifold, Ch(f)=1/2∫|∇f|²dm, a quadratic form. Quadraticity is expressed by the parallelogram identity

Ch(f+g)+Ch(f−g)=2Ch(f)+2Ch(g).

The Δ of experiment ② tests this identity on a single pair of covectors at one point. When this parallelogram identity holds, polarization E(f,g)=[Ch(f+g)−Ch(f−g)]/4 gives a bilinear energy on the finite-energy domain D(Ch)={f∈L²:Ch(f)<∞}. This quadraticity is the extra RCD requirement, not an automatic property of Cheeger energy. The L² subdifferential ∂Ch(u) is the set of vectors g such that Ch(v)≥Ch(u)+⟨g,v−u⟩L² for every v. The gradient flow follows u′(t)∈−∂Ch(u(t)). For the ordinary Laplacian Δg=divg∇, take the unweighted measure m=volg; on its L² operator domain D(Δg), ∂Ch(u)={−Δgu} and the flow is ∂ₜu=Δgu. For a weighted measure m=e−ψvolg, the corresponding operator is Δm=Δg−⟨∇ψ,∇·⟩. The identity with the Laplacian applies only on its operator domain, not to every finite-energy function. If Ch is quadratic, its subdifferential is linear on that domain, so the heat flow preserves sums. Existence and uniqueness of the subdifferential flow are analytic theorems used here. For example, in a normed vector space the local slope of a smooth function is the dual norm |df|*. If the original norm is not induced by an inner product, its square is generally not a quadratic form in df, so Cheeger energy need not be quadratic.

Definition map. In the AGS convention, RCD(K,∞) combines strong CD(K,∞) with quadratic Cheeger energy. Strong CD means that for each endpoint pair there is one optimal path measure Π such that entropy convexity also holds along every normalized bounded nonnegative reweighting FΠ. It lies between ordinary CD, which asks for one suitable interpolation, and convexity along every transport geodesic; the latter is not the definition of strong CD. The fact that RCD implies suitable all-geodesic convexity is a separate deep equivalence theorem. Finite-dimensional RCD(K,N) adds the same quadraticity to a chosen finite-dimensional CD formulation. Entropic and reduced conventions vary, so a theorem should name its convention.

Here the endpoint pairs are the admissible absolutely continuous, finite-entropy measures in P₂(X). The weight F is a bounded Borel function on Geo(X), and its normalization is ∫F dΠ=1.

The distinction is real: a finite-dimensional vector space with a non-Euclidean norm and Lebesgue measure can satisfy a suitable CD(0,N) condition. If the norm is not induced by an inner product, its Cheeger energy is generally not quadratic, so it is not RCD. CD alone therefore does not force a Riemannian limit; RCD encodes the Hilbertian structure associated with linear heat flow.

The equivalences between entropy gradient flow, linear heat flow, and Bochner inequalities on singular spaces are deep theorems. We explain the definition and why it is added, but do not claim to prove those analytic equivalences here. They use relaxation of Cheeger energy, gradient-flow theory, and optimal-transport differential formulas.

What is proved here and what is cited?

We connect the geometry of Wasserstein geodesics, the Riccati calculation for a smooth geodesic family, and the change of variables from Jacobians to entropy. In the finite-dimensional section, the pointwise comparison from RicN≥K to the distance-distortion coefficient is cited as a smooth comparison theorem; we work out how it integrates to the Rényi bound. Existence and almost-everywhere regularity of optimal maps, endpoint versions of the distortion comparison, and the converse from finite-dimensional CD to weighted Ricci bounds are used as theorems. They are not presented as elementary lemmas proved on this page.

Equivalence of CD(K,N) and reduced CD*(K,N) can require assumptions such as nonbranching. Finite and infinite dimensional RCD, and entropic and reduced versions, also have distinct formulations. Before applying a theorem, check completeness, the support and local finiteness of the measure, absolute continuity of endpoints, branching assumptions, and the quantifier in the chosen definition.

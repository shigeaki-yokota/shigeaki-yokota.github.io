Topics / Probability and geometry

A Gaussian view of high-dimensional spheres

Pick a point at random on a 100-dimensional sphere, and it will almost certainly lie close to the “equator”, whichever direction you call north. Magnify the small leftover wobble by √n, and the coordinates start to follow the Gaussian bell curve. What survives if we look beyond coordinates, at every possible observation? Starting from concentration on the sphere, this page works up to the question answered by Shioya's pyramid convergence.

Starting here. The prerequisites are linear algebra and the expectation and variance of random variables. Detailed calculations sit in collapsible boxes, and the proof of the theorem is collected at the end. The two experiments update as you move the sliders or press the buttons. Each one comes with a question to answer before you touch it; guessing first makes the surprise easier to notice.
The theorem followed here (Shioya, Theorem 1.1(3))
Give the n-dimensional sphere of radius rn its ambient Euclidean (chord) distance and normalized area measure. If rn/√n→λ>0, its associated pyramids converge weakly to the virtual Gaussian pyramid of variance λ2. We first prove the case λ=1, then restore the scale.

1 / Choosing the scale

Magnify a wobble that vanishes on the unit sphere by √n

The question. What happens when you choose a point at random on a high-dimensional sphere? Start with the simplest observation there is: its “height”, the first coordinate.

Take an ordinary sphere, like the surface of the Earth, choose a point uniformly with respect to area, and read off its height x1. As Archimedes showed, on the unit sphere the height is uniformly distributed on [−1,1]. So the band |x1|≤0.1 around the equator catches exactly 10% of the area. Now measure the same band on unit spheres of higher dimension.

Share of the area of the unit n-sphere lying in the equatorial band |x1|≤0.1
Dimension n2101001000
Share in band10%about 24%about 68%about 99.8%

The band never gets wider, yet as the dimension grows almost all of the area pours into it. And because any direction can play the role of “height”, most of the area sits near every equator at once. It is hard to picture from low-dimensional drawings, but the calculation leaves no room for doubt.

Where the numbers in the table come from

Slicing the unit sphere Sn(1)⊂Rn+1 at height t gives an (n−1)-sphere of radius √(1−t2), whose area is proportional to (1−t2)(n−1)/2. Moving the height by dt moves a distance dt/√(1−t2) along the sphere, so the height x1 has density

proportional to (1−t2)(n−2)/2　(−1<t<1).

For n=2 the exponent is 0 and we recover Archimedes' uniform distribution. The table integrates this density numerically. The 68% at n=100 almost matches the probability that a standard normal variable lies in [−1,1] (about 68.3%), because the band width 0.1 is exactly 1/√100. That coincidence previews the √n magnification below.

Coordinates are not the only observations that pile up. Consider any observation whose reading changes by no more than the distance you move the point: think of a gauge whose needle never moves faster than the point does. To make this precise we first fix some vocabulary.

Let Sn(r)={x∈Rn+1:|x|=r} be the n-dimensional sphere of radius r>0, with uniform distribution given by area divided by total area. A sphere carries two natural distances: the chord distance |x−y|, measured along straight lines in the surrounding space, and the geodesic distance, the length of the shortest path along the sphere. Pyramid convergence later on uses the chord distance.

For a distance d, a function f is 1-Lipschitz if |f(x)−f(y)|≤d(x,y) for every pair of points. The first coordinate x↦x1 is the typical example. Let X be a uniform point on the unit sphere. Lévy's concentration phenomenon says that such observations become nearly constant as the dimension grows. Precisely, if n≥2 and f:Sn(1)→R is 1-Lipschitz for the geodesic distance, then for every t>0

P(|f(X)−mf|>t) ≤ 2 exp(−(n−1)t2/2).

Here mf is a median: a value with P(f(X)≤mf)≥1/2 and P(f(X)≥mf)≥1/2. The estimate follows from Lévy's isoperimetric inequality (Shioya, Theorem 5.3, §5). Since the chord distance never exceeds the geodesic distance, the same estimate applies to functions that are 1-Lipschitz for the chord distance. To put numbers in: for n=100, f(x)=x1 (median 0) and t=0.3, the right side is about 2.3%. The true probability is about 0.22%, so the bound is crude but points the right way.

Why the isoperimetric inequality gives an exponential bound

Lévy's isoperimetric inequality says that among closed sets of a given area, a spherical cap (a ball for the geodesic distance) has the slowest-growing neighbourhoods. The closed set {f≤mf} has at least half the area, and its t-neighbourhood is contained in {f<mf+t}. So the upper tail has probability at most the area outside the t-neighbourhood of a hemisphere. The lower tail is the same.

With latitude u measured from the equator, the latitudinal factor of the area element is cosn−1u. For 0<t<π/2, the fraction of area left on one side is

∫tπ/2cosn−1u du / (2∫0π/2cosn−1u du) ≤ (1/2)cosn−1t ≤ (1/2)e−(n−1)t²/2.

The first inequality substitutes u=t+v and uses cos(t+v)≤cos t cos v; the second follows from log cos t≤−t2/2. Adding both tails gives a bound stronger than the one in the text. For t≥π/2 the tail area is 0. This page takes the isoperimetric inequality itself as a known theorem.

Does the wobble disappear altogether? No: it only shrinks to size about 1/√n. Setting t=s/√n above shows that the scale controlling the bound is n−1/2. Not every observation fluctuates that much, but the coordinate x↦x1 does. Rotational symmetry and ∑Xi2=1 give mean 0 and variance 1/(n+1). For n=100 the standard deviation is about 0.1, the width of the band in the table.

So we magnify until the wobble is visible again. Loosely speaking, we turn up the microscope's magnification to √n as the dimension grows. Mathematically, we multiply distances on the sphere by √n, which is the same as moving the unit sphere to the sphere of radius √n by x↦√n x. For an observation f on the unit sphere,

g(y)=√n f(y/√n),　y∈Sn(√n)

is still 1-Lipschitz after magnification, and its fluctuations are √n times larger. The concentration bound now has a size independent of the dimension, and the variance of the first coordinate becomes n/(n+1)→1. Next we check that the shape of this surviving wobble is Gaussian.

2 / Building the distribution

Keep the Gaussian direction and adjust only its length

Why bring in a Gaussian? The coordinates of a point on a sphere are tied together by the constraint that their squares add up to a constant, which makes them awkward to handle one at a time. A vector of independent normal variables has coordinates you can treat separately, and its direction has no preference. So we first build a Gaussian vector and then fix only its length.

Let G=(G1,…,Gn+1) have independent standard normal coordinates, each with density φ(t)=(2π)−1/2e−t²/2, mean 0 and variance 1. Put R=|G|.

The joint density (2π)−(n+1)/2e−|x|²/2 depends only on |x|, not on direction. In polar coordinates the volume element is rndr dω, so density times volume splits into a radial factor rne−r²/2dr and the uniform spherical area element dω. Radius and direction are therefore independent, and the direction is uniform on the unit sphere. Consequently,

U = (√n / R) G　∈　Sn(√n)

is exactly uniform on the sphere of radius √n. No approximation is involved: passing from the Gaussian to the sphere changes the length and keeps the direction.

Check uniformity and independence in polar coordinates

The probability that the radius lies in [a,b] and the direction in a spherical set A is, for a constant Cn,

Cn∫abrne−r²/2dr · area(A).

A radial integral times a directional area means the joint probability is the product of the two probabilities: that is independence. The direction has probability area(A)/area(Sn(1)), which is exactly the uniform distribution.

This construction explains the coordinate limit. The sum R2 of the independent Gi2 clusters around its mean n+1, and R/√n→1 in probability. In other words, the factor √n/R that carries the Gaussian direction onto the sphere tends to 1. As a rule of thumb, R/√n typically differs from 1 by about 0.07 when n=100 and by about 0.007 when n=10000.

Show from mean and variance that the relative radial error vanishes

Write E for expectation and Var for variance. First compute the mean and variance of R2=∑Gi2.

Second and fourth moments by integration by parts

Let Z be standard normal. Its density satisfies φ′(x)=−xφ(x). Since xmφ(x)→0 at both ends, integration by parts for even m≥2 gives

E[Zm]=∫xmφ(x)dx=(m−1)∫xm−2φ(x)dx=(m−1)E[Zm−2].

Starting from E[1]=1 we get E[Z2]=1 and E[Z4]=3, hence Var(Z2)=3−12=2. Variances of independent variables add, so R2 has mean n+1 and variance 2(n+1).

Our sphere has radius √n, so we measure the deviation of R2 from n rather than from its mean n+1. Expanding the variance gives

E[(R2−n)2]=Var(R2)+(E[R2]−n)2=2(n+1)+1=2n+3.

For x=R2/n≥0 we have √x+1≥1, so

|√x−1|=|x−1|/(√x+1)≤|x−1|.

Square, take expectations, and substitute the previous identity.

E[(R/√n − 1)2] ≤ (2n+3)/n2 → 0
From a mean-square bound to probability tending to one

For nonnegative W, a·1{W≥a}≤W. Taking expectations gives Markov's inequality P(W≥a)≤E[W]/a. With W=(R/√n−1)2,

P(|R/√n−1|≥ε)≤(2n+3)/(n2ε2)→0　(ε>0).

This is exactly convergence in probability. The probability that R/√n is close to 1 grows, but we have not claimed that the displacement R−√n, measured in units of distance, becomes small.

What becomes small is the radial relative error. Next we see how concentration in a shell that is thin compared with the radius √n affects a fixed, small number of coordinates.

3 / The coordinate limit

A fixed number of coordinates stays Gaussian

The question. If the factor √n/R is close to 1, the first few coordinates of the sphere point U should be almost the same as the original Gaussian coordinates. Is that really so? Change the dimension and see.

Fix k≥1. The first k coordinates of the sphere point U=(√n/R)G converge, as n→∞, to the k-dimensional standard Gaussian distribution. This is the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution law (Shioya, Proposition 5.1, §5). Because the factor tends to 1, the difference from the original Gaussian coordinates vanishes as long as we look at only a few of them.

Convergence of distributions means that the average of every bounded continuous function converges to its average under the limit distribution; this is also called weak convergence.

The left plot below shows the distribution of U1 approaching the standard Gaussian bell curve. The right plot shows R−√n, the absolute displacement of the original Gaussian radius. The coordinate error vanishing and this radial difference vanishing are two different things.

The flat shape at n=2 on the left is Archimedes' uniform distribution from the first section, magnified to radius √2. For the right plot, here is a rough estimate. The standard deviation of R2 is √(2(n+1))≈√(2n). Since R is the square root of R2, near R2≈n a deviation of R2 becomes a deviation of R about 1/(2√n) times as large. Multiplying gives √(2n)/(2√n)=1/√2≈0.71, and n drops out. Indeed E[(R−√n)2] tends to 1/2 as n→∞ (the readout's theoretical value is about 0.698 at n=2 and about 0.707 at n=160). The shell gets thin compared with its radius, but its thickness stays around 0.7. This “thickness that does not shrink” is the subject of Sections 5 and 6.

Building U from the same Gaussian vector makes the comparison more concrete. Write E for expectation, U1:k=(U1,…,Uk) and G1:k=(G1,…,Gk). For n≥2 and 1≤k≤n+1,

E[|U1:k−G1:k|2] ≤ 3k/n.

The left side is the mean squared distance between the two points in the same trial. The radial difference |R−√n| (about 0.7) is spread thinly over n+1 coordinates, so each coordinate carries only a small share. For fixed k the bound tends to 0, so the sphere coordinates and the Gaussian coordinates also become close in distribution. When all coordinates are compared at once, k=n+1, the bound does not tend to 0.

The coordinate-error estimate, and on to convergence in distribution

Since U and G are built on the same probability space, their full distance is just the radial difference.

|U−G|=|√n−R|,　　(R−√n)2=(R2−n)2/(R+√n)2≤(R2−n)2/n.

The last inequality uses R+√n≥√n. Next, rotating the pair (U,G) simultaneously does not change its distribution, so every coordinate has the same mean squared error. All n+1 of them add up to the full error, so the first k contribute the fraction k/(n+1).

E[∑i=1k(Ui−Gi)2]=k/(n+1)·E[|U−G|2].

Substitute the distance bound and E[(R2−n)2]=2n+3.

E[ |(U1,…,Uk) − (G1,…,Gk)|2 ] ≤ k(2n+3)/(n(n+1)) ≤ 3k/n.
From this estimate to convergence in distribution

For every ε>0, Markov's inequality bounds the probability that the two k-vectors are more than ε apart by 3k/(nε2). So in this coupling the distance between the sphere coordinates and the Gaussian vector tends to 0 in probability. In particular, for any bounded 1-Lipschitz function f (one with |f(x)−f(y)|≤|x−y| for all x,y),

|E f(U1:k)−E f(G1:k)|≤E|U1:k−G1:k|≤√(3k/n).

Convergence of the averages of all bounded Lipschitz functions is equivalent to convergence in distribution on Rk. So for fixed k, the first k coordinates of the sphere converge to the standard Gaussian distribution. Shioya [Proposition 5.1] also states convergence of the densities themselves.

4 / Comparing all observations

Does every observation become normal?

The question. Coordinates are just one example of 1-Lipschitz observations. What shows up in the limit for the others?

There are many other observations that do not stretch distances. For instance, y↦|y1| is 1-Lipschitz, and its limit is the distribution of |Z| for a standard normal variable Z. It takes only nonnegative values, so it is not a normal distribution. A Gaussian appearing on the sphere does not mean that every observation follows the same bell curve.

For real-valued observations there is a sharper answer. Let fn:Sn(√n)→R be 1-Lipschitz, and suppose that along some subsequence the observed values fn(U) of a uniform sphere point converge weakly to a probability distribution ν. Then ν is the distribution of α(Z) for some 1-Lipschitz function α:R→R (Shioya, Theorem 5.2, §5). In the |y1| example, α(z)=|z|. So as long as we read a single real number, all we can see in the limit is “one standard normal variable, processed by a function that does not stretch distances”. The theorem is stated for the geodesic distance and therefore applies to the chord distance too. We quote this real-valued theorem as a known result; the proof of pyramid convergence below does not use it.

What if we include observations that read two or more values at once, or that take values in a general metric space? Here we change viewpoint. Instead of comparing the sphere itself with something, we make a catalogue of every observation result that can be produced from the sphere without stretching distances, and we compare catalogues. Each entry of the catalogue pairs “a set of values with a distance” with “the distribution obtained by choosing an original point at random and mapping it”.

A metric together with a probability measure is called an mm-space. The collection PX records every mm-space obtained from X by a 1-Lipschitz map, where the target carries the distribution of the image of the random original point. This is the associated pyramid of X, the catalogue just described. For the sphere Sn(√n), the catalogue contains the real line carrying the distribution of the first coordinate, the half-line carrying the distribution of |y1|, the space Rk carrying the distribution of the first k coordinates, and so on.

Shioya's Theorem 1.1(3) compares these whole records. The associated pyramids of spheres of radius √n converge to the virtual Gaussian pyramid, which collects the observations obtainable from all finite-dimensional Gaussian spaces together with their limits. This does not regard the usual infinite-product Gaussian as a single mm-space with the ℓ² distance (the square root of the sum of squares): an infinite sequence of independent standard normal variables has a divergent sum of squares with probability 1 (Shioya, Introduction, §4). The limiting “space” does not exist, but the limiting “catalogue” does. That is what “virtual” means here.

The two directions the proof needs
Projecting to coordinates puts every observation of a finite-dimensional Gaussian into the limit. Conversely, clipping a Gaussian onto a slightly smaller sphere without stretching distances shows that the sphere's limit contains nothing more.

This convergence does not claim that all the dimension-dependent observations correspond uniformly to one fixed finite-dimensional Gaussian model. The precise definition of weak convergence and the two inclusions are in the final proof. Before that, we see why normalizing to the original radius is not enough and a “slightly smaller sphere” is needed.

5 / The obstacle in the reverse comparison

The relative radial error vanishes, but the distance error does not

Why care? The second half of the proof goes the other way: it wants to produce the sphere from the Gaussian without stretching distances. The obstacle is the “bump that does not get narrower” from the right plot in Section 3.

In numbers: for n=100 the Gaussian radius R is roughly 10±0.7, and for n=10000 roughly 100±0.7. The shell becomes thin compared with its radius, but its thickness of about 0.7 stays. Thin is not the same as zero thickness.

U lies on the sphere of radius √n, while G spreads through the whole surrounding space. The earlier coordinate error becomes small only when we look at k coordinates; with k=n+1 its right side does not become small. So we cannot conclude from it that the two measures approach each other in the same Euclidean space.

The thickness matters if we try to place the Gaussian points exactly on the sphere of radius √n. By the central limit theorem, (R2−(n+1))/√(2(n+1)) approaches the standard normal distribution, so P(R<√n)→1/2. For n=10, 100, 1000 this probability is about 0.47, 0.49 and 0.497. In other words, about half of the points lie inside the sphere, and the only way to put them on it is to push them outward. Pushing outward can stretch distances. As an extreme case, take two points εu and −εu (|u|=1) pointing in opposite directions just next to the origin. They are 2ε apart, but after the normalization x↦√n x/|x| to length √n they are 2√n apart. The smaller ε, the larger the stretching factor.

The argument that follows keeps this radial difference and compares only observations that do not increase distances (1-Lipschitz observations).

Check that absolute radial fluctuations do not vanish

The polar volume element gives the radius density qn(r)=cnrne−r²/2, with a normalizing constant cn>0. Its maximum is at r=√n. For s≥0,

qn(√n+s)/qn(√n)=(1+s/√n)ne−√n s−s2/2.

Set t=s/√n and use log(1+t)≥t−t2/2. The logarithm of this ratio is at least −s2, so

qn(√n+s) ≥ qn(√n)e−s².

Integrate over s≥0. Total probability 1 gives 1≥qn(√n)∫0∞e−s²ds=qn(√n)√π/2, hence qn(√n)≤2/√π. The density on [√n−1/4,√n+1/4] is at most its maximum, so

P(|R−√n|≤1/4) ≤ 1/√π.

Thus with probability at least 1−1/√π (about 0.43), the radius differs from √n by more than 1/4. Relative fluctuations vanish; absolute fluctuations in distance do not.

6 / Building the reverse comparison

Keep the inside of the ball and pull only the outside onto its boundary

The idea. If pushing outward is the problem, do not push. Make the target sphere slightly smaller, of radius θ√n with θ<1. Then nearly every point lies outside it and only needs to be pulled in, and pulling in never stretches distances.

As the previous section showed, normalizing a Gaussian directly onto the sphere, x↦√n x/|x|, can pull points near the origin far apart, so it is not 1-Lipschitz. Instead we clip to the closed ball of radius a>0: points inside stay where they are, and only the points outside are pulled radially onto the boundary sphere.

Ta(x) = x　(|x|≤a),　　Ta(x)=a x/|x|　(|x|>a)

This map is 1-Lipschitz for the chord distance. Write the points as ru and sv with unit vectors u,v and radii r,s≥0; the squared distance is (r−s)2+rs|u−v|2. The first term is the radial difference. The second comes from the difference in direction: like the ribs of a folding fan, which are closest near the pivot, two points at a given angle are closer the nearer they are to the origin. After the map the radii become min(r,a) and min(s,a), and neither the radial difference nor the angular term increases.

Prove the 1-Lipschitz property term by term

Write x=ru, y=sv, with r,s≥0 and |u|=|v|=1; at the origin any direction will do. Expanding the inner product gives

|ru−sv|2=(r−s)2+rs|u−v|2.

Put r′=min(r,a), s′=min(s,a). The function t↦min(t,a) has slope 0 or 1, so |r′−s′|≤|r−s|, and also r′s′≤rs. Therefore

|Ta(x)−Ta(y)|2=(r′−s′)2+r′s′|u−v|2≤|x−y|2.

Both sides are nonnegative, so taking square roots preserves the inequality. No pair of points is pulled farther apart.

Now fix 0<θ<1 and take the clipping radius a=θ√n in each dimension. Since we showed R/√n→1 in probability,

pn:=P(R<θ√n)→0.

More explicitly, E[R2]=n+1, Var(R2)=2(n+1) and n+1−θ2n≥(1−θ2)n, so Chebyshev's inequality gives

pn≤2(n+1)/((1−θ2)2n2).

With probability 1−pn, a Gaussian point lies outside the clipping ball and Ta moves it onto the sphere of radius θ√n. Only the exceptional interior mass stays inside. The bound grows as θ approaches 1 and becomes meaningless at θ=1. What the experiment showed, that θ=0.99 still leaves many exceptions at n=1000, is the numerical face of this.

The idea in one line
Exact radial agreement is unnecessary. A nonexpanding contraction onto a slightly smaller sphere catches almost all Gaussian points.

The clipped distribution is a 1-Lipschitz observation of Gaussian space, and it differs from the uniform distribution on the sphere of radius θ√n only by the exceptional probability pn. This supports the reverse inclusion. First fix θ<1 and let n→∞; only then let θ↑1. With θ=1 from the start, R/√n→1 alone does not make the interior probability vanish, and the bound above is unavailable. In fact, as the previous section showed, the interior probability for θ=1 tends to 1/2.

7 / Definitions and proof

Identify the limiting pyramid by two inclusions

Coordinate projections put the Gaussian observations into the limit; the clipping map keeps everything else out. From here on we prove both directions along the definition of weak convergence of pyramids. Besides the normalization, coordinate and clipping calculations, we use, with citations, the compactness of the space of pyramids and the continuity of rescaling.

Why compare catalogues of observations rather than the spaces themselves?

Before the definitions, here are three reasons for the change of viewpoint made in Section 4.

  1. There is no common stage. Sn(√n) lives in Rn+1, a different space for each dimension, so there is no common place to lay points on top of each other. Observation results, such as distributions on the real line, can be placed in the same space whatever dimension they come from.
  2. The candidate limit does not exist as a space. As noted in Section 4, an infinite sequence of independent standard normal variables has a divergent sum of squares and does not form a single mm-space with the ℓ² distance. Yet every observation obtained from a finite-dimensional Gaussian is an ordinary mm-space.
  3. Our calculations were facts about observations all along. The distribution obtained by coordinate projection (Section 3) and the image of the clipping map (Section 6) both answer the question “what can a map that does not stretch distances produce?”. In the language of catalogues, the two calculations become the two directions of the proof.

As an analogy, when two solid objects of different shapes cannot be laid on top of each other, one can compare the bundles of shadows they cast from every direction. In mathematics, a “shadow” has to be fixed as the image under a map that does not stretch distances, and closeness of shadow bundles must be defined, before this becomes a comparison. The definitions below do exactly that.

mm-spaces and push-forward

An mm-space consists of a complete (Cauchy sequences converge, so the metric has no holes) and separable (countably many points approximate it densely) metric space (X,d) and a probability measure μ on its Borel sets, the family generated by open sets. For a Borel map f:X→Y, the transported measure is f*μ(B)=μ(f−1(B)): sample an original point at random and record its image.

Nonexpanding maps

Y≺X means there is a 1-Lipschitz map from X to Y that does not stretch distances and transports the probability measure exactly to that of Y. Mind the direction: the larger space X produces the observation Y. Example: the real line carrying the distribution of the first coordinate is smaller than Sn(√n) in the sense of ≺.

Associated pyramid

PX={Y:Y≺X} is the collection of all mm-spaces obtainable from X in this way. It is closed for the box distance defined below. Rather than the space itself, it records the possible observations that do not stretch distances.

Finite-dimensional Gaussian spaces

Let γk be the joint distribution of k independent standard normal variables and Γk=(Rk,|·|,γk) the standard Gaussian space. Projection to the first k coordinates is 1-Lipschitz, so Γk≺Γk+1, and the associated pyramids grow with the dimension.

Box distance and weak convergence of pyramids

To compare catalogues we first need a ruler for their entries, that is, for how close two mm-spaces are. Picture comparing the tables of distances between towns on two maps. We may discard regions of small area; we match up the remaining locations and check how well the tables agree for every pair of locations.

The box distance □ between two mm-spaces measures how uniformly the distances between all pairs of points can be matched, after discarding a small-probability exception. □(X,Q) is the infimum of the box distances between the mm-space X and members of a pyramid Q: how well X can be approximated by spaces in Q.

In general a pyramid is a nonempty family of mm-spaces that (i) contains every Y with Y≺X whenever it contains X, (ii) contains, for any two members, a member dominating both, and (iii) is closed for the box distance. The associated pyramid PX satisfies these conditions. The virtual Gaussian pyramid is defined as

PΓ∞ = closure□(⋃k≥1PΓk)

where closure□ is the closure for the box distance. It collects the observations obtained from finite-dimensional Gaussian spaces and adds their limits (Shioya, Definition 2.19; §4, pp.13–14).

What does it mean for a sequence of catalogues to converge to a catalogue? As with convergence of a sequence of sets, we require two things: what is in the limit is nearby, and what is not in the limit stays away. Weak convergence of pyramids Qn to Q has two tests. (i) Every mm-space in the limit can be approximated by members of Qn. (ii) Every mm-space outside the limit stays a positive distance from Qn. These two-sided conditions define convergence of the whole record of observations.

The first inclusion uses (ii): coordinate projections approach the Gaussian spaces, so those spaces cannot lie outside a subsequential limit of the spheres. The second inclusion uses (i) and the closedness of the Gaussian pyramid: a space in the limit of clipped Gaussians can be approximated by spaces in the Gaussian pyramid, so it stays inside.

Box distance and weak convergence, with parameters and formulas

Represent each mm-space by a Borel parameter, a map sending uniform measure on [0,1) to its probability measure. The box distance □ chooses two parameters and a Borel set A⊂[0,1) so that the distances for all pairs are uniformly close, and asks how small the discarded probability and the distance error can be made. Precisely, it is the infimum of ε≥0 with Leb(A)≥1−ε and |dX(φ(s),φ(t))−dY(ψ(s),ψ(t))|≤ε for all s,t∈A, where Leb is uniform measure on [0,1). The distance to a pyramid Q is □(X,Q)=infY∈Q□(X,Y).

Qn converges weakly to Q if (i) □(X,Qn)→0 for every X∈Q, and (ii) lim infn→∞□(X,Qn)>0 for every X∉Q. The formal definitions are Shioya [Definitions 2.13, 2.14, 2.19, 2.20].

Preparation: close measures give close pyramids

Prokhorov distance dP between probability measures μ,ν on the same metric space measures closeness allowing small movements and small mass exceptions. Shioya's Propositions 2.15, 2.18 and Theorem 1.2 give

ρ(P(X,μ),P(X,ν))≤dconc((X,μ),(X,ν))≤□((X,μ),(X,ν))≤2dP(μ,ν).

From left to right: pyramid distance ρ, observable distance, box distance, and Prokhorov distance of measures. The last inequality uses the same underlying metric space X. Closeness of clipped Gaussian and sphere measures therefore gives closeness of their pyramids.

First inclusion: the limit contains the Gaussian pyramid

  1. Extract a limit. The space Π of pyramids is compact for weak convergence (Shioya, Theorem 2.21; Proposition 3.5). If sphere pyramids did not converge to the target, a subsequence nj→∞ would stay a fixed positive distance away and admit another limit P: PSnj(√nj)⇀P.
  2. Project coordinates. Projection to the first k coordinates is 1-Lipschitz and sends sphere measure to μnj,k. Our calculation gives μnj,k⇒γk. In finite dimension, weak convergence is convergence in Prokhorov distance. Proposition 2.15 therefore gives □((Rk,μnj,k),Γk)→0.
  3. Apply weak convergence. Each projected space belongs to its sphere's pyramid. Thus Γk∈P. Otherwise condition (ii) would keep it a positive box distance from the sphere pyramids, contradicting the distance tending to zero.

Every Γk belongs to P. Downward and box closedness give

PΓ∞⊂P.

Second inclusion: the limit is no larger than the Gaussian pyramid

Scale each mm-space's metric by θ without changing its measure to define θP={(X,θd,μ):(X,d,μ)∈P}.

First note the following closedness property. If pyramids Qn⊂G, with G box-closed, converge weakly to Q, then Q⊂G. Indeed, X∈Q outside G would have □(X,G)>0, hence the same positive lower bound for □(X,Qn), contrary to condition (i) (Shioya, Definition 2.20).

  1. Fix the clipping radius. Fix θ∈(0,1) and set aj=θ√nj. Since Taj is 1-Lipschitz, observations from the clipped Gaussian are observations from the original Gaussian.
  2. Only exceptional mass contributes measure error. For Gaussian radius Rj in Rnj+1, let pj=P(Rj<aj)→0. With probability 1−pj, clipping yields a uniform point on the radius-aj sphere. Replace the exceptional interior point by an independent uniform sphere point. This couples the measures with disagreement probability at most pj, so dP≤pj. The prepared chain makes their pyramid distance tend to zero.
  3. Restore the scale last. The sphere pyramids with radius √nj converge to P, so those with radius θ√nj converge to θP (Shioya, Lemma 6.2). Let Qj be the clipped Gaussian pyramids. The chain gives ρ(Qj,PSnj(θ√nj))→0; thus Qj⇀θP by the triangle inequality. Nonexpansion gives Qj⊂PΓ∞, so the preceding closedness property gives θP⊂PΓ∞.

This holds for every θ<1. Choose θj↑1. Lemma 6.2 gives θjP⇀P, and the same closedness property yields P⊂PΓ∞. Both inclusions give equality, contradicting the choice of a different limit. Hence the entire sphere-pyramid sequence converges weakly to the Gaussian pyramid.

Write the exceptional-mass comparison as a formula

Let μn=(Ta)*γn+1 and let σa be uniform sphere measure of radius a. Independence of radius and direction makes the projected exterior distribution σa. With interior probability pn and conditional interior distribution νn,

μn=(1−pn)σa+pnνn.

This mixture couples the two measures identically outside probability pn, giving dP(μn,σa)≤pn directly from the Prokhorov definition.

Generalize to radii rn/√n→λ

Put λn=rn/√n. Then Sn(rn)=λnSn(√n), meaning metric scaling. A variance-λ2 Gaussian is the standard Gaussian with distances scaled by λ. From λn→λ>0 and Shioya's Lemma 6.2, continuity of scaling for weak convergence gives

PSn(rn) ⇀ PΓ∞λ².

This is Theorem 1.1(3), for chord distance. Kazukawa [Theorem 1.7] proves the corresponding result for standard spherical Riemannian distance by another metric-transform method.

Topics / Probability and geometry

A Gaussian view of high-dimensional spheres

A Gaussian vector has a uniformly distributed direction. Rescaling its length to √n gives an exactly uniform point on the sphere of radius √n. In large dimension, a few coordinates of this point approach a Gaussian distribution. Does the whole sphere approach a Gaussian space too?

Starting here. The prerequisites are linear algebra, expectation and variance of random variables, and basic proof experience. Integration by parts is reviewed in the calculations. The first half follows the probability calculations step by step; the second separates the proof of Shioya's Theorem 1.1(3) into definitions, lemmas, and two inclusions. We state and cite the structural results needed, rather than rebuilding all of metric measure geometry.
The theorem followed here (Shioya, Theorem 1.1(3))
Give the n-dimensional sphere of radius rn its ambient Euclidean (chord) distance and normalized area measure. If rn/√n→λ>0, its associated pyramids converge weakly to the virtual Gaussian pyramid of variance λ2. We first prove the case λ=1, then restore the scale.

1 / Starting point

Keep the Gaussian direction and adjust only its length

Let Sn(r) be the sphere of radius r in Rn+1. Let G=(G1,…,Gn+1) have independent standard normal coordinates, each with density φ(t)=(2π)−1/2e−t²/2. Expectation E[X] is the mean, and variance Var(X)=E[(X−E[X])2] is the mean squared deviation. Put R=|G|.

The joint density (2π)−(n+1)/2e−|x|²/2 does not depend on direction. In polar coordinates, volume is rndr dω, so density times volume splits into a radial factor rne−r²/2dr and uniform spherical area dω. Radius and direction are independent, and direction is uniform on the unit sphere. Consequently,

U = (√n / R) G　∈　Sn(√n)

is exactly uniform on the sphere of radius √n. This step is exact. Passing from Gaussian to sphere changes length while keeping direction.

Check uniformity and independence in polar coordinates

The probability that radius lies in [a,b] and direction in a spherical set A is, for a constant Cn,

Cn∫abrne−r²/2dr · area(A).

The product of a radial integral and a directional area gives the product of the event probabilities: independence. Direction has probability area(A)/area(Sn(1)), precisely uniform distribution.

To study radial concentration, first compute the mean and variance of R2=∑Gi2. Expand the calculation of the second and fourth normal moments below.

Derive second and fourth moments by integration by parts

The standard normal density satisfies φ′(x)=−xφ(x). Since xmφ(x)→0 at both ends, integration by parts for even m≥2 gives

E[Zm]=∫xmφ(x)dx=(m−1)∫xm−2φ(x)dx=(m−1)E[Zm−2].

Starting with E[1]=1, obtain E[Z2]=1 and E[Z4]=3, hence Var(Z2)=3−12=2. Independent variances add, giving mean n+1 and variance 2(n+1) for R2.

Our sphere has radius √n, so compare with n rather than the mean n+1. Expanding variance gives

E[(R2−n)2]=Var(R2)+(E[R2]−n)2=2(n+1)+1=2n+3.

For x=R2/n≥0, use √x+1≥1:

|√x−1|=|x−1|/(√x+1)≤|x−1|.

Square, take expectations, and substitute the preceding identity.

E[(R/√n − 1)2] ≤ (2n+3)/n2 → 0
Pass from a mean square bound to probability tending to one

For nonnegative W, a·1{W≥a}≤W. Taking expectations gives Markov's inequality P(W≥a)≤E[W]/a. With W=(R/√n−1)2,

P(|R/√n−1|≥ε)≤(2n+3)/(n2ε2)→0　(ε>0).

This is convergence in probability. The probability of R/√n near 1 grows, but we have not shown that displacement R−√n in distance units becomes small.

Thus the radial relative error becomes small. Gaussian points concentrate in a shell thin relative to radius √n.

Why does error in the first k coordinates vanish? Since U and G are constructed on the same probability space, their full distance is just radial difference.

|U−G|=|√n−R|,　　(R−√n)2=(R2−n)2/(R+√n)2≤(R2−n)2/n.

The last inequality uses R+√n≥√n. Rotational symmetry of U and G makes mean squared coordinate errors equal. All n+1 sum to full error, so the first k contribute the fraction k/(n+1).

E[∑i=1k(Ui−Gi)2]=k/(n+1)·E[|U−G|2].

Substitute the distance bound and E[(R2−n)2]=2n+3:

E[ |(U1,…,Uk) − (G1,…,Gk)|2 ] ≤ k(2n+3)/(n(n+1)) ≤ 3k/n.
From this estimate to convergence in distribution

For every ε>0, Markov's inequality bounds the probability that the two k-vectors are more than ε apart by 3k/(nε2). In this coupling their distance tends to zero in probability. For any bounded 1-Lipschitz function f, satisfying |f(x)−f(y)|≤|x−y|,

|E f(U1:k)−E f(G1:k)|≤E|U1:k−G1:k|≤√(3k/n).

Convergence of integrals of all bounded Lipschitz functions is equivalent to convergence in distribution on Rk. Thus for fixed k, the sphere's first k coordinates converge to standard Gaussian distribution. Shioya [Proposition 5.1] also states convergence of the densities themselves.

This concerns observations of a fixed finite number of coordinates, not comparison of all coordinates at once.

3 / What this gives

Gaussian behavior survives coordinate observation

The coupling using one vector compares the first k coordinates of a uniform sphere point with a standard Gaussian k-vector in mean square. For fixed k, the error vanishes. Densities also become Gaussian, described in Shioya's paper as the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution law. Convergence in distribution means convergence of averages measured by bounded continuous functions.

The phenomenon
The whole point lies on the sphere of radius √n, yet a fixed finite set of coordinates looks like independent standard Gaussians.

This says what is visible in a few coordinates. Rotational symmetry allows another choice of coordinate directions, but does not make all directions and coordinates close simultaneously.

4 / An essential boundary

Sphere and Gaussian measures need not be close in the same ambient space

U lies on the sphere of radius √n, while G spreads through ambient space. The coordinate estimate becomes small only when observing k coordinates. Substituting k=n+1 does not make its right side small. It therefore does not imply that the measures approach each other in the same Euclidean space.

In fact the Gaussian radial width around √n does not vanish in distance units. A shell thin relative to its radius need not have thickness zero. We next compare distance-nonincreasing (1-Lipschitz) observations while retaining this radial difference.

Check that absolute radial fluctuations do not vanish

Polar volume gives the radius density qn(r)=cnrne−r²/2, with cn>0. Its maximum is at r=√n. For s≥0,

qn(√n+s)/qn(√n)=(1+s/√n)ne−√n s−s2/2.

Set t=s/√n and use log(1+t)≥t−t2/2. The logarithm of this ratio is at least −s2, so

qn(√n+s) ≥ qn(√n)e−s².

Integrate over s≥0. Total probability 1 gives 1≥qn(√n)∫0∞e−s²ds=qn(√n)√π/2, hence qn(√n)≤2/√π. Bounding density by its maximum on [√n−1/4,√n+1/4],

P(|R−√n|≤1/4) ≤ 1/√π.

Thus with probability at least 1−1/√π (about 0.43), radius differs from √n by more than 1/4. Relative fluctuations vanish; absolute distance fluctuations do not.

Keep these separate. Finite-coordinate convergence, closeness of measures in one ambient space, and pyramid convergence are different claims, each requiring its own argument.

5 / The decisive map

Contract only the exterior toward a sphere

Direct Gaussian normalization to a sphere may greatly separate points near the origin, making it unsuitable as a nonexpanding observation. Instead leave the ball of radius a unchanged and contract only its exterior to the sphere.

Ta(x) = x　(|x|≤a),　　Ta(x)=a x/|x|　(|x|>a).

1-Lipschitz means that for all x,y, |Ta(x)−Ta(y)|≤|x−y|. Writing points as ru, sv, squared distance is (r−s)2+rs|u−v|2. Replacing radii by min(r,a), min(s,a) enlarges neither the radial difference nor the angular term.

Prove nonexpansion by separating the two terms

Write x=ru, y=sv, with r,s≥0 and |u|=|v|=1. At the origin choose any direction. Expanding the inner product gives

|ru−sv|2=(r−s)2+rs|u−v|2.

Put r′=min(r,a), s′=min(s,a). The function t↦min(t,a) has slope 0 or 1, so |r′−s′|≤|r−s|, and r′s′≤rs. Therefore

|Ta(x)−Ta(y)|2=(r′−s′)2+r′s′|u−v|2≤|x−y|2.

Both sides are nonnegative, so square roots preserve the inequality. No pair of points is pulled farther apart.

Fix a=θ√n, 0<θ<1. Since R/√n→1 in probability,

pn:=P(R<θ√n)→0.

More explicitly, for large n, n+1−θ2n≥(1−θ2)n. Chebyshev gives

pn≤2(n+1)/((1−θ2)2n2).

With probability 1−pn, Gaussian points lie outside the clipping ball and Ta moves them to radius θ√n. Only the exceptional interior mass stays inside.

The idea
Exact radial agreement is unnecessary. A nonexpanding contraction onto a slightly smaller sphere captures almost all Gaussian points.

6 / The meaning of convergence

From close coordinates to a limit of spaces

This is the paper's geometric part. We compare spaces carrying metrics and probabilities together. First establish notation, then split the proof into “contains the Gaussian pyramid” and “contains nothing beyond it.”

mm-spaces and push-forward

An mm-space consists of a complete (Cauchy sequences converge) separable (countably many points approximate it densely) metric space (X,d) and a probability measure μ on its Borel sets, generated by open sets. For a Borel map f:X→Y, the transported measure is f*μ(B)=μ(f−1(B)): sample an original point and record its image.

Nonexpanding maps

Y≺X means there is a 1-Lipschitz map from X to Y transporting its probability exactly to that of Y. The direction matters: the larger space X produces observation Y.

Associated pyramid

PX collects all mm-spaces obtainable this way and is closed under box-distance limits. It records possible nonexpanding observations of a space.

The finite-dimensional Gaussian candidate

Let Γk=(Rk,|·|,γk) be standard Gaussian space. The paper's PΓ∞ is the box closure of the union of PΓk for k=1,2,…. This is a pyramid, not the usual infinite-dimensional Gaussian probability space itself.

The definitions of box distance and weak convergence

Represent each mm-space by a Borel parameter sending uniform measure on [0,1) to its probability measure. Box distance □ chooses two parameters and a Borel A⊂[0,1) to make distances for all pairs uniformly close, while minimizing discarded probability and distance error. Precisely, take the infimum of ε≥0 with Leb(A)≥1−ε and |dX(φ(s),φ(t))−dY(ψ(s),ψ(t))|≤ε for all s,t∈A. Here Leb is uniform measure on [0,1). Set □(X,Q)=infY∈Q□(X,Y).

Weak convergence of Qn to Q has two conditions: (i) each X∈Q approaches some member of Qn in box distance; (ii) each space outside Q stays a positive distance away. Both inclusions and exclusions matter.

Formal definitions: Shioya [Definitions 2.13, 2.14, 2.19, 2.20]. We extract the forms used in the proof.

Preparation: close measures give close pyramids

Prokhorov distance dP between probability measures μ,ν on the same metric space measures closeness allowing small movements and small mass exceptions. Shioya's Propositions 2.15, 2.18 and Theorem 1.2 give

ρ(P(X,μ),P(X,ν))≤dconc((X,μ),(X,ν))≤□((X,μ),(X,ν))≤2dP(μ,ν).

From left to right: pyramid distance ρ, observable distance, box distance, and Prokhorov distance of measures. The last inequality uses the same underlying metric space X. Closeness of clipped Gaussian and sphere measures therefore gives closeness of their pyramids.

First inclusion: the limit contains the Gaussian pyramid

  1. Extract a limit. The space Π of pyramids is compact for weak convergence (Shioya, Theorem 2.21; Proposition 3.5). If sphere pyramids did not converge to the target, a subsequence nj→∞ would stay a fixed positive distance away and admit another limit P: PSnj(√nj)⇀P.
  2. Project coordinates. Projection to the first k coordinates is 1-Lipschitz and sends sphere measure to μnj,k. Our calculation gives μnj,k⇒γk. In finite dimension, weak convergence is convergence in Prokhorov distance. Proposition 2.15 therefore gives □((Rk,μnj,k),Γk)→0.
  3. Apply weak convergence. Each projected space belongs to its sphere's pyramid. Thus Γk∈P. Otherwise condition (ii) would keep it a positive box distance from the sphere pyramids, contradicting the distance tending to zero.

Every Γk belongs to P. Downward and box closedness give

PΓ∞⊂P.

Second inclusion: the limit is no larger than the Gaussian pyramid

Scale each mm-space's metric by θ without changing its measure to define θP={(X,θd,μ):(X,d,μ)∈P}.

First note the following closedness property. If pyramids Qn⊂G, with G box-closed, converge weakly to Q, then Q⊂G. Indeed, X∈Q outside G would have □(X,G)>0, hence the same positive lower bound for □(X,Qn), contrary to condition (i) (Shioya, Definition 2.20).

  1. Fix the clipping radius. Fix θ∈(0,1) and set aj=θ√nj. Since Taj is 1-Lipschitz, observations from the clipped Gaussian are observations from the original Gaussian.
  2. Only exceptional mass contributes measure error. For Gaussian radius Rj in Rnj+1, let pj=P(Rj<aj)→0. With probability 1−pj, clipping yields a uniform point on the radius-aj sphere. Replace the exceptional interior point by an independent uniform sphere point. This couples the measures with disagreement probability at most pj, so dP≤pj. The prepared chain makes their pyramid distance tend to zero.
  3. Restore the scale last. The sphere pyramids with radius √nj converge to P, so those with radius θ√nj converge to θP (Shioya, Lemma 6.2). Let Qj be the clipped Gaussian pyramids. The chain gives ρ(Qj,PSnj(θ√nj))→0; thus Qj⇀θP by the triangle inequality. Nonexpansion gives Qj⊂PΓ∞, so the preceding closedness property gives θP⊂PΓ∞.

This holds for every θ<1. Choose θj↑1. Lemma 6.2 gives θjP⇀P, and the same closedness property yields P⊂PΓ∞. Both inclusions give equality, contradicting the choice of a different limit. Hence the entire sphere-pyramid sequence converges weakly to the Gaussian pyramid.

Write the exceptional-mass comparison as a formula

Let μn=(Ta)*γn+1 and let σa be uniform sphere measure of radius a. Independence of radius and direction makes the projected exterior distribution σa. With interior probability pn and conditional interior distribution νn,

μn=(1−pn)σa+pnνn.

This mixture couples the two measures identically outside probability pn, giving dP(μn,σa)≤pn directly from the Prokhorov definition.

Generalize to radii rn/√n→λ

Put λn=rn/√n. Then Sn(rn)=λnSn(√n), meaning metric scaling. A variance-λ2 Gaussian is the standard Gaussian with distances scaled by λ. From λn→λ>0 and Shioya's Lemma 6.2, continuity of scaling for weak convergence gives

PSn(rn) ⇀ PΓ∞λ².

This is Theorem 1.1(3), for chord distance. Kazukawa [Theorem 1.7] proves the corresponding result for standard spherical Riemannian distance by another metric-transform method.

What was derived here. Normalization, moments, coordinate errors, clipping, and measure comparison were derived on the page. The remaining background inputs are metrization of finite-dimensional weak convergence by Prokhorov distance, compactness of pyramid space (Shioya, Theorem 2.21 / Proposition 3.5), and continuity of scaling (Lemma 6.2). The full theorem's proof connects them in the order above.
